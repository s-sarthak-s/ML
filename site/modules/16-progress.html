Module 16 · Beyond text

Why models keep getting better

GPT-3 (2020) was trained with about 3×10²³ FLOPs on 300B tokens. Llama 3.1 405B (2024) used about 4×10²⁵ FLOPs on roughly 15T tokens, and Llama 3 8B, a model 20 times smaller than GPT-3, scores about 66% on MMLU against GPT-3's 44%. The gains came from several places at once: more compute, more and cleaner data, better recipes, post-training, and letting models think longer when they answer. This module pulls those levers apart and puts a rough number on each. Where a number is an estimate, it says so.

By the end you can

  1. Split a capability gain into compute, data, algorithms, post-training, and test-time compute, and turn a growth rate into a doubling time.
  2. Read a scaling-law plot, and explain how a smoothly falling loss can still produce a sudden jump on a benchmark.
  3. Plan a pretraining data mixture: how much repetition is safe, and what filtering and synthetic data buy and risk.
  4. Compute best-of-N and majority-vote accuracy from a per-sample success rate, and say when voting makes things worse.
  5. Weigh the open arguments about data limits, cost, and diminishing returns without overclaiming either side.

Three levers, then two more

"Models got better because they got bigger" is true but incomplete. Here is a more useful accounting. The first three levers act during pretraining, and they multiply rather than add:

Lever 1 · compute

More FLOPs

More accelerators, faster accelerators, longer runs, and less idle time. The easiest to measure and historically the biggest contributor.

Lever 2 · data

More and better tokens

Training sets grew from hundreds of billions of tokens to tens of trillions. Filtering and mixing them well matters about as much as the count.

Lever 3 · algorithms

More loss per FLOP

Architecture, optimizer, schedules, precision, kernels, and the compute-optimal recipe itself. Epoch AI treats these as a multiplier on compute.

After pretraining come two levers that barely existed as separate fields in 2020. Post-training (SFT, preference tuning, RL with verifiable rewards; Module 10) turns a document-completer into an assistant and, more recently, a reasoner. Test-time compute spends extra FLOPs per question: longer chains of thought, several samples with a vote, or a verifier that picks the best one.

To compare training runs across years, you need one number that folds in both hardware and recipe quality. That number is effective compute.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{C_{\text{eff}}} = \htmlClass{t2}{F_{\text{peak}}} \times \htmlClass{t3}{n_{\text{GPU}}} \times \htmlClass{t4}{T} \times \htmlClass{t5}{u} \times \htmlClass{t6}{m_{\text{alg}}}$$
\(C_{\text{eff}}\)
effective compute, in FLOPs of some older reference recipe that would give the same model quality
\(F_{\text{peak}}\)
peak FLOP/s per accelerator (H100 bf16 dense ≈ 989 TFLOP/s)
\(n_{\text{GPU}}\)
number of accelerators in the run
\(T\)
wall-clock training time in seconds
\(u\)
utilization, i.e. model FLOPs utilization (MFU, Module 07), typically 0.3–0.5
\(m_{\text{alg}}\)
algorithmic multiplier: how many reference-recipe FLOPs one of today's FLOPs is worth

In wordsThe first four factors give the physical FLOPs you actually burned. The last one credits you for using them better than an older recipe would have.

Why it mattersA sanity check on Llama 3.1 405B: Meta reports about 30.8M H100-hours at 38–43% MFU. 30.8×10⁶ h × 3600 s × 989×10¹² × 0.38 ≈ 4.2×10²⁵ FLOPs, close to the 6ND estimate of 6 × 405×10⁹ × 15.6×10¹² ≈ 3.8×10²⁵. The algorithmic multiplier is the part nobody can measure directly. It has to be inferred by comparing many models over time.

The levers aren't independent

More compute is only useful with more data (Chinchilla's point, Module 07). Better filtering changes how many tokens you need. Post-training and test-time compute work better on stronger base models: RL can only reinforce behaviours the model sometimes produces. So "how much came from data versus compute" has no clean answer. The sections below give each lever's mechanism and the best available estimates, and they say where the estimates are soft.

Compute: the steepest curve

Epoch AI keeps a database of notable ML models with estimated training compute. Their analyses find that compute for frontier models has grown by roughly 4–5× per year since about 2010 (Sevilla et al., 2022; Epoch AI's 2024 update estimates about 4.1× per year across notable models, 90% CI 3.7–4.6×, and closer to 5× per year for frontier language models since 2020). Treat these as estimates. The exact rate depends on which models count as frontier, and many labs don't publish their training compute.

Where does 4–5× per year come from? Roughly, from three multiplying sources. Hardware price-performance improves by about 1.3–1.4× per year. Spending on the largest runs has grown faster: Cottier et al. (2024) estimate the amortized hardware and energy cost of frontier training runs grew about 2.4× per year since 2016. Runs also got longer and clusters bigger. None of these can grow at today's rate forever, which is why the last section of this module is about limits.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{C(t)} = \htmlClass{t2}{C_0}\,\htmlClass{t3}{g}^{\,\htmlClass{t4}{t}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t5}{T_{2}} = \frac{\ln 2}{\ln \htmlClass{t3}{g}}, \qquad \text{orders of magnitude per year} = \log_{10} \htmlClass{t3}{g}$$
\(C(t)\)
compute of the largest run at time \(t\)
\(C_0\)
compute today
\(g\)
growth factor per year (Epoch estimate: about 4–5)
\(t\)
years from now
\(T_2\)
doubling time in years

In wordsMultiply by \(g\) every year. At 4× per year the doubling time is \(\ln 2/\ln 4 = 0.5\) years, so six months. At 5× it's 0.43 years, about 5.2 months. Either way you gain 0.6–0.7 orders of magnitude per year, so 6–7 per decade.

Why it mattersMoore's-law-style hardware gains double every two years or so. Frontier training compute has doubled about four times as often, which means most of the growth came from spending and scale, not from faster chips.

Think first: if physical compute grows 4× per year and algorithms halve the compute needed every 8 months, how fast does effective compute grow?
The algorithmic factor per year is \(2^{12/8} = 2^{1.5} \approx 2.8\). Effective compute grows about \(4 \times 2.8 \approx 11\times\) per year, doubling roughly every 3.5 months. That headline is only as good as its inputs, and the algorithmic estimate has a wide confidence interval (section 9). Set the widget to 4× and 8 months to see it.

Scaling laws, and why abilities seem to appear suddenly

Module 07 covered Kaplan et al. (2020) and Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) in detail. The short version: pretraining loss falls as a smooth power law in compute, across many orders of magnitude, approaching a floor set by the entropy of text itself. Here we only need the one-variable form.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{L(C)} = \htmlClass{t2}{L_\infty} + \htmlClass{t3}{a}\,\htmlClass{t4}{C}^{-\htmlClass{t5}{\alpha}}$$
\(L(C)\)
pretraining loss (nats per token) of a compute-optimal model trained with \(C\) FLOPs
\(L_\infty\)
irreducible loss: no model can beat the randomness of the text
\(a\)
scale constant fitted to small runs
\(C\)
training compute in FLOPs
\(\alpha\)
exponent, small: Kaplan et al. found loss ∝ \(C^{-0.05}\) without a floor; fits with a floor give somewhat larger values

In wordsEach 10× of compute shrinks the reducible part of the loss by the same fraction, \(10^{-\alpha}\). On a log-log plot of \(L - L_\infty\) against \(C\), that's a straight line.

Why it mattersThe same power law reads as two opposite stories. Optimists see a straight line that keeps going. Pessimists see that with \(\alpha = 0.05\), halving the reducible loss takes \(2^{1/0.05} = 2^{20} \approx 10^6\) times more compute. Both are reading the same curve correctly.

Emergence: smooth loss, sharp benchmarks

Wei et al. (2022) catalogued "emergent abilities": tasks where small models score near zero and, past some scale, accuracy shoots up, for example multi-digit arithmetic and some BIG-bench tasks. If loss improves smoothly, where do the jumps come from?

Schaeffer, Miranda and Koyejo (2023) argued that many of them come from the metric. Exact-match scoring gives credit only if every token of the answer is right. If the model gets each token right with probability \(p\), roughly independently, the answer is right with probability \(p^k\):

$$\htmlClass{t1}{P(\text{exact match})} \approx \htmlClass{t2}{p}^{\,\htmlClass{t3}{k}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t2}{p} = 1 - \htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon(C)}$$
\(P(\text{exact match})\)
benchmark score under exact-match grading
\(p\)
per-token accuracy on the answer tokens
\(k\)
number of tokens that all have to be right (answer length)
\(\varepsilon(C)\)
per-token error, which falls smoothly (e.g. as a power law) with compute

In wordsA small, steady improvement in each token becomes a big swing in the product. With \(k = 20\), moving \(p\) from 0.80 to 0.95 takes exact match from 1% to 36%.

Why it mattersBefore calling something emergent, re-score it with a continuous metric (per-token accuracy, log-probability of the right answer, edit distance). If that curve is smooth, you can forecast the jump instead of being surprised by it.

Both sides of the emergence debate

Schaeffer et al. showed that switching to continuous metrics made most of the emergent curves they examined smooth, and that small test sets add to the effect: a 1-in-10,000 success rate reads as exactly zero on 100 questions. They did not claim no ability ever appears abruptly. Later work (for example Du et al., 2024) found some tasks where performance stays near chance until pretraining loss drops below a threshold, even under continuous metrics. The practical rule holds either way. Track continuous metrics alongside the headline score, because they give you warning.

How much repetition is safe?

Muennighoff et al. (2023) trained hundreds of models with a fixed budget of unique tokens repeated for different numbers of epochs. Their headline: up to about 4 epochs, repeated data was almost as good as fresh data; beyond that returns fell quickly, and by around 16 epochs extra passes were worth very little. They fit a simple model of how much a repeated token is worth:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{D'} = \htmlClass{t2}{U} + \htmlClass{t2}{U}\,\htmlClass{t3}{R^{*}}\left(1 - e^{-\htmlClass{t4}{R}/\htmlClass{t3}{R^{*}}}\right)$$
\(D'\)
effective data: the number of fresh tokens that would give the same loss
\(U\)
unique tokens available
\(R\)
number of repeats, i.e. epochs − 1
\(R^*\)
fitted decay constant, about 15.4 in their experiments: after roughly that many repeats, more passes add little

In wordsThe first pass is worth full price. Each extra pass is worth a bit less than the last, decaying exponentially. No amount of repetition gets you past about \(U(1+R^*) \approx 16U\).

Why it mattersPlug in numbers: 4 epochs (\(R = 3\)) gives \(D' \approx 3.7U\), 93% as good as 4U of fresh data. 16 epochs gives about \(10.6U\) (66%). 40 epochs gives about \(15.2U\) (38%). The fit comes from models up to 9B parameters, so treat the constant as indicative, not universal.

The data wall

Villalobos et al. (2024, Epoch AI) estimate the stock of quality- and repetition-adjusted public human-generated text at about 300 trillion tokens, with a 90% interval of roughly 100T to 1,000T. If training sets keep growing at their recent pace, they project that frontier runs will use up that stock somewhere between 2026 and 2032. These numbers are estimates built on many assumptions about how much of the web is usable, but you can see why the arithmetic is uncomfortable:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{t_{\text{wall}}} = \frac{\ln\left(\htmlClass{t2}{S} / \htmlClass{t3}{D_0}\right)}{\ln \htmlClass{t4}{g_D}}$$
\(t_{\text{wall}}\)
years until the largest training set equals the whole stock
\(S\)
stock of usable text (Epoch estimate: ~300T tokens, 90% CI 100T–1,000T)
\(D_0\)
today's largest training set (15–36T tokens in 2024–25)
\(g_D\)
yearly growth factor of training-set size (an assumption; 2–3× is in the historical range)

In wordsHow many times must the dataset multiply to reach the stock, measured in years of growth.

Why it mattersWith \(S/D_0 = 20\) and \(g_D = 2.5\): \(\ln 20 / \ln 2.5 \approx 3.3\) years. Make the stock 1,000T and you get about 4.6 years. Repeating the data 4 times (×3.7 effective) buys only \(\ln 3.7/\ln 2.5 \approx 1.4\) more years. Exponential demand eats any fixed multiplier quickly.

Why the data wall may be softer than it looks

The estimate covers public human text. It excludes private and licensed data, transcribed audio and video, and synthetic data, and it assumes today's filtering standards. On the other side, the most valuable text (textbooks, high-quality code, careful reasoning) is a small fraction of the stock and gets used first. Which effect dominates is genuinely unsettled.

Data quality and curation

Raw web text is mostly menus, cookie banners, SEO spam, duplicates, and machine-generated junk. Turning a crawl into a training set is a pipeline of increasingly expensive filters. Each stage throws data away, and that's the point: at a fixed token budget, removing bad tokens means making room for better ones.

Heuristics, dedup, decontamination

The cheap stages are rules: drop pages under a word count, pages where lines rarely end in punctuation, pages full of symbols or repeated n-grams, pages in the wrong language according to a fastText language-ID model (the C4 and Gopher rule sets are the classic references). Deduplication follows: exact hashes for copies, MinHash for near-copies. Lee et al. (2022) showed dedup reduces memorization and improves training efficiency. Decontamination removes documents that overlap benchmark test sets, typically by n-gram matching (Module 13). Skipping it inflates your evals without improving the model.

Model-based quality filters

The stage that changed results most in 2024 was using a model to judge quality. FineWeb-Edu (Penedo et al., 2024) asked Llama-3-70B-Instruct to rate about 450K web pages for educational value on a 0–5 scale, trained a small regression head on embeddings to imitate those ratings, then ran it over all 15T tokens. Models trained on the filtered 1.3T did clearly better on knowledge and reasoning benchmarks such as MMLU and ARC than models trained on the same number of unfiltered tokens. DCLM (Li et al., 2024) compared many filters and found a plain fastText classifier worked best, with positives drawn from instruction data (OpenHermes 2.5) and highly upvoted r/ExplainLikeImFive answers. A 7B model trained on 2.6T tokens of its output reached 64% on MMLU (5-shot).

Pitfall · your filter defines "quality"

A classifier trained to find "educational" pages will also drop fiction, casual dialogue, dialects, and niche technical forums. Filters tuned to raise MMLU partly select text that resembles MMLU. That's useful, but it's also a quiet form of teaching to the test. Always read samples of what got removed, not only what was kept.

Mixtures and annealing

The final dataset is a mixture of domains with chosen weights. Meta reports Llama 3's final pretraining mix as roughly 50% general knowledge, 25% math and reasoning, 17% code, and 8% multilingual. Small domains (math, curated code, books) are usually upsampled, which means repeated. That's where the 4-epoch guidance from the previous section bites.

Many recipes also anneal: near the end of training, as the learning rate decays to zero, they shift the mix toward the highest-quality sources. The Llama 3 report says annealing on a small amount of high-quality code and math data gave large gains for the 8B model on GSM8K and MATH, but negligible gains for the 405B. They also used short annealing runs as a cheap test of whether a new dataset was worth adding.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{e_i} = \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{w_i}\,\htmlClass{t3}{D}}{\htmlClass{t4}{U_i}}, \qquad \sum_i \htmlClass{t2}{w_i} = 1$$
\(e_i\)
how many times domain \(i\)'s unique tokens are seen
\(w_i\)
mixture weight: share of training tokens drawn from domain \(i\)
\(D\)
total training-token budget
\(U_i\)
unique tokens available in domain \(i\) after filtering

In wordsA domain's weight times the total budget, divided by what you actually have. Above 1 means repetition.

Why it mattersPut 25% math into a 15T-token run with 0.8T unique math tokens and you repeat it about 4.7 times, already past the comfortable range. The widget below shows this tension.

Synthetic data and model collapse

When you run short of good human data, one option is to generate it. "Synthetic data" covers several different things, with very different risk profiles:

Distillation

A stronger model writes, a weaker one learns

Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2022) bootstrapped instruction data from a model's own outputs; most open instruction sets since then are generated by stronger models. The DeepSeek-R1 small models were trained this way on R1's reasoning traces (Module 10).

Rejection sampling

Generate many, keep what checks out

Sample several solutions, keep those whose final answer matches or whose code passes tests, and train on them. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) did this for reasoning; Llama 3 used execution feedback to filter synthetic code.

Textbook-quality data

Write the data you wish existed

Phi-1 (Gunasekar et al., 2023): a 1.3B model trained on about 6B tokens of filtered web code plus about 1B tokens of GPT-3.5-written textbooks and exercises reached 50.6% pass@1 on HumanEval, far above other models its size at the time.

Verified generation

Math, code, and proofs with a checker

When a program can verify an answer, synthetic data can be unlimited and correct. AlphaGeometry (Trinh et al., 2024) trained on 100 million synthetic geometry theorems and proofs. Rephrasing web text into cleaner styles also helps (Maini et al., 2024).

The pattern: synthetic data works best when something independent of the generator checks it, whether a unit test, a proof checker, a known answer, or a stronger model. Without a check, you're training a model on its own guesses.

Model collapse

Shumailov et al. (2024, Nature) showed what happens when each generation of a model is trained only on samples from the previous one. The rare events go first, since a finite sample tends to miss them, so the next model assigns them even less probability. Over generations the distribution narrows and drifts, and the tails never come back. The cleanest illustration fits a Gaussian, samples from the fit, and refits:

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\htmlClass{t1}{\hat\sigma^2_{t+1}} \,\middle|\, \htmlClass{t3}{\sigma^2_{t}}\right] = \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{n}-1}{\htmlClass{t2}{n}}\,\htmlClass{t3}{\sigma^2_{t}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbb{E}\!\left[\htmlClass{t1}{\hat\sigma^2_{\htmlClass{t4}{t}}}\right] = \left(1-\frac{1}{\htmlClass{t2}{n}}\right)^{\htmlClass{t4}{t}} \sigma^2_0$$
\(\hat\sigma^2_{t+1}\)
variance the next generation learns, using the maximum-likelihood estimate (divide by \(n\))
\(\sigma^2_t\)
variance of the current generation's model
\(n\)
number of samples each generation trains on
\(t\)
generation number

In wordsEach refit loses a little spread on average, and the losses compound. Individual runs also wander randomly, and once a run's spread gets small, nothing pushes it back.

Why it mattersWith \(n = 100\), the expected variance after 100 generations is \(0.99^{100} \approx 0.37\) of the original. Real models collapse through the same channel: finite samples underrepresent the tails, plus fitting error.

The fix is boring: keep the real data

Gerstgrasser et al. (2024) showed that if each generation accumulates synthetic data alongside the original real data, instead of replacing it, the error stays bounded in their experiments. In the widget, set "real data mixed in" to even 10% and watch the chains stop collapsing. Labs use synthetic data as a targeted supplement with filtering, not as a closed loop.

Post-training: the same base model, much more useful

A base model completes documents. Ask it a question and it may continue with three more questions, as if it were writing a quiz. Post-training (Module 10) teaches it to answer, follow formats, use tools, refuse appropriately, and, more recently, to reason at length. The striking thing is how little compute this takes relative to pretraining and how much it changes.

InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., 2022) is the cleanest early evidence. Human labelers preferred outputs from a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT over the 175B GPT-3 base model, despite a 100× size gap. The paper reports training costs of about 60 petaflop/s-days for the 175B RLHF model, against about 3,640 for GPT-3's pretraining: under 2%.

Three things happen during post-training, and they are easy to conflate:

  • Elicitation. Most capabilities are already in the base model. SFT on tens of thousands of good examples teaches it which behaviour you want and in what format.
  • Preference shaping. RLHF and DPO push toward answers people (or AI judges) rate higher: more helpful, better calibrated refusals, more readable. It's also the stage that can introduce sycophancy if the signal rewards agreement.
  • New skills through RL. RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR, Module 10) on math and code problems, as in DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025), produced long chains of thought with self-checking and backtracking. These behaviours were rare in the base model, and RL made them reliable.
MLE angle · post-training's share is growing

In 2022, post-training was a rounding error in the compute budget. Reasoning models changed that: OpenAI's o1 announcement (2024) reported performance rising smoothly with more RL training compute, and labs now describe RL runs as a major line item. Public numbers are scarce, so treat any specific split as a guess. For your own work, the lesson is that the cheapest capability gain is often better post-training data on an existing base, not a bigger base.

Test-time compute

The levers so far all happen before deployment. Test-time compute spends more FLOPs on each question. The simplest form is chain-of-thought prompting (Wei et al., 2022): letting the model write out intermediate steps measurably improves arithmetic and multi-step reasoning. Beyond that, you can sample more than once.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{P_{\text{best-of-}N}} = 1 - \left(1 - \htmlClass{t2}{p}\right)^{\htmlClass{t3}{N}}$$
\(P_{\text{best-of-}N}\)
probability at least one of the samples is correct, and the verifier picks it
\(p\)
probability a single sample is correct (assumed independent across samples)
\(N\)
number of samples

In wordsYou only fail if every sample fails. With \(p = 0.2\) and \(N = 10\): \(1 - 0.8^{10} \approx 0.89\). This is the same quantity as pass@k in Module 13.

Why it mattersA good verifier turns a mediocre generator into a good system. A bad one doesn't: if the verifier also accepts wrong answers, accuracy plateaus at the rate it picks right ones. Learned reward models can be exploited this way (Gao et al., 2022, on reward-model overoptimization).

$$\htmlClass{t1}{P_{\text{maj}}} = \sum_{\htmlClass{t2}{j}=\lceil \htmlClass{t3}{N}/2 \rceil}^{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \binom{\htmlClass{t3}{N}}{\htmlClass{t2}{j}}\, \htmlClass{t4}{p}^{\,\htmlClass{t2}{j}} (1-\htmlClass{t4}{p})^{\htmlClass{t3}{N}-\htmlClass{t2}{j}}$$
\(P_{\text{maj}}\)
probability the majority answer is correct
\(j\)
number of correct samples; we need more than half
\(N\)
number of samples (odd, so there are no ties)
\(p\)
per-sample accuracy

In wordsThe tail of a binomial: the chance that more than half the votes are right. Condorcet's jury theorem says that as \(N\) grows this goes to 1 if \(p \gt 0.5\) and to 0 if \(p \lt 0.5\).

Why it mattersVoting amplifies whatever the model usually does. On questions it usually gets wrong, voting makes it reliably wrong. Free-form answers soften this, because wrong answers scatter across many values while right answers agree.

Reasoning models

OpenAI's o1 (2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) moved test-time compute into the model itself. Trained with RL on problems with checkable answers, they learn to write long chains of thought, try approaches, notice errors, and backtrack. Both reports show accuracy rising with the number of thinking tokens. The R1 paper also shows response length growing steadily during RL training without being told to.

Snell et al. (2024) asked how to spend a fixed test-time budget. Their answer was "adaptively": easy questions need one sample or a quick revision, hard ones benefit from search against a verifier. A compute-optimal allocation beat best-of-N by more than 4× in efficiency in their setup. On problems where a small model already has some success, extra test-time compute could outperform a model about 14× larger at matched FLOPs. On the hardest problems, more pretraining was still the better buy.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{C_{\text{query}}} \approx 2\,\htmlClass{t2}{N_{\text{active}}} \times \htmlClass{t3}{T_{\text{out}}} \times \htmlClass{t4}{S}$$
\(C_{\text{query}}\)
FLOPs to answer one question (ignoring prefill and attention)
\(N_{\text{active}}\)
parameters used per token (all of them for dense models; only the routed experts for MoE)
\(T_{\text{out}}\)
output tokens per sample, including hidden reasoning tokens
\(S\)
number of samples drawn

In wordsTwo FLOPs per active parameter per generated token, times every token you generate.

Why it mattersA 70B dense model writing a 300-token answer: \(2 \times 70\text{B} \times 300 \approx 4\times10^{13}\) FLOPs. The same model thinking for 10,000 tokens: \(1.4\times10^{15}\), 33× more, before you sample 16 times. Test-time compute moves cost from a one-off training bill to every request, which changes serving economics (Module 13).

Why it mattersOver 4 years the central estimate gives \(2^{48/8} = 64\times\) less compute. The confidence interval runs from about 11× (\(\tau = 14\)) to about 780× (\(\tau = 5\)). Quote this number with its range or not at all.

What the 8-month estimate does and doesn't measure

It's measured on perplexity, on older benchmarks, mostly for pre-2023 models. It doesn't capture post-training, test-time compute, or data-quality gains on modern evaluations. Some algorithmic gains only appear at scale, and some small-scale tricks vanish at scale, so a single rate is a simplification. Ho et al. also conclude that compute scaling contributed more of the total improvement than algorithms over that period.

Why small models got so good

The gap between frontier and small models narrowed sharply. A few concrete reasons, mostly covered elsewhere in the course:

  • Overtraining. Llama 3 8B saw about 15T tokens, nearly 100× the Chinchilla ratio. A smaller model trained longer costs more to train than it "should" but less every time it's used (Sardana et al., 2023).
  • Better data. Everything in the curation section helps small models disproportionately, because they can't afford to spend capacity on junk. Phi-1's result is an extreme example.
  • Distillation. Gemma 2's 2B and 9B models were trained with knowledge distillation from a larger teacher (Gemma Team, 2024). Meta used logits from Llama 3.1 8B and 70B as targets when pretraining Llama 3.2 1B and 3B. DeepSeek distilled R1's reasoning into Qwen and Llama models from 1.5B to 70B.
  • Better post-training, which transfers almost unchanged to small bases.

The result, phrased carefully: on many standard benchmarks, 2024-era models in the 3–9B range match or beat models 20–50× larger from 2020–2022. Microsoft's Phi-3 report (Abdin et al., 2024) claims its 3.8B model rivals GPT-3.5 on benchmarks such as MMLU.

Where small models still lose

Benchmark scores on popular tests are where contamination and targeted data have the most effect, so headline comparisons flatter small models. Factual recall still scales with parameters: Allen-Zhu and Li (2024) estimate language models store about 2 bits of knowledge per parameter in their synthetic setups. Long multi-step tasks, rare languages, and obscure facts are where you'll feel the difference. Test on your workload (Module 13).

Evals steer, feedback loops accelerate

Evals as the steering wheel

Labs optimize what they measure. A benchmark follows a predictable lifecycle: released as hard, hill-climbed, saturated, partly contaminated, replaced. Module 13 covers contamination and saturation in depth. Here is the pattern as it played out:

BenchmarkIntroduced asWhat happened
MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020)57-subject knowledge exam; GPT-3 scored about 44%Frontier models above 85% by 2024; label errors now limit it. Successors: MMLU-Pro, GPQA.
GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021)Grade-school math word problemsSaturated; a fresh look-alike set (GSM1k, Zhang et al., 2024) found some models scored noticeably lower, which points to overfitting.
GPQA (Rein et al., 2023)Graduate-level science questions written to resist web searchClimbed quickly with reasoning models.
FrontierMath, Humanity's Last Exam (2024–25)Research-level problems, mostly unpublishedBuilt to last longer by keeping test items private.
SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., 2023)Real GitHub issues, graded by the repo's testsBecame a target for agentic coding, with a human-verified subset added to fix bad items.

Arenas (LMArena, Chiang et al., 2024) measure crowd preferences on prompts users choose, which is closer to real use but is moved by style, length, and formatting. Every eval is a proxy, and Goodhart's law applies: once a proxy is optimized hard, it drifts from what it was meant to measure. The response is a steady supply of harder, private, or time-split evals, plus your own task evals.

Feedback loops

A deployed model generates the raw material for its successor. That's the flywheel, and it's a real driver of progress beyond the three pretraining levers:

  • User preference data: thumbs up/down, side-by-side comparisons, and edits in products like ChatGPT become reward-model training data.
  • Agentic environments: repositories with tests, browsers, terminals. The model attempts a task, the environment scores it, and RL learns from the result. This is RLVR applied to multi-step work.
  • Self-play and search: AlphaGo Zero (Silver et al., 2017) learned Go only from games against itself. Language has no single win condition, but math and code partly do, so similar loops are possible there.
  • AI feedback: Constitutional AI (Bai et al., 2022) used a model's own critiques against written principles as the preference signal (RLAIF), reducing the need for human labels.
Pitfall · the loop optimizes the signal, not your intent

In April 2025 OpenAI rolled back a GPT-4o update that had become sycophantic. Their postmortem said an added reward signal based on users' thumbs-up/down feedback was one of the contributing factors. Any feedback loop needs evals for the behaviours you don't want, run before each release.

A useful habit

When you read a claim that progress is accelerating or stalling, ask which lever it's about (compute, data, algorithms, post-training, test-time), which metric it uses (loss, benchmark, arena, field study), and whether the number is measured or estimated. Most public arguments get louder by blurring those three things.

Hands-on

1. Fit a scaling law, then distrust the extrapolation

Fit both a pure power law and one with a floor to toy "small runs", then extrapolate 1,000× beyond the data. The bootstrap band only covers noise in the points. It says nothing about whether the functional form holds out there.


import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
C = np.logspace(17, 20, 7)                        # 7 small runs, 1e17..1e20 FLOPs
true = lambda c: 1.8 + 1.94 * (c / 1e17) ** -0.07
L = true(C) + rng.normal(0, 0.01, C.size)         # toy eval losses with noise

# (1) no floor: log L = log a - alpha * log C, a straight line in log-log space
slope, icpt = np.polyfit(np.log10(C), np.log10(L), 1)
print(f"pure power law: L ~ C^{slope:.3f}")

# (2) with an irreducible floor: L = L_inf + A * (C/1e17)^-alpha
def law(c, L_inf, A, alpha):
    return L_inf + A * (c / 1e17) ** -alpha

bounds = ([0, 0, 0], [5, 10, 1])
p, _ = curve_fit(law, C, L, p0=(1.5, 2.0, 0.1), bounds=bounds)
print("fit: L_inf=%.2f  A=%.2f  alpha=%.3f" % tuple(p))

# (3) extrapolate 1000x past the data with a residual bootstrap
target = 1e23
fitted = law(C, *p)
resid = L - fitted
preds = []
for _ in range(500):
    Lb = fitted + rng.choice(resid, resid.size, replace=True)
    try:
        pb, _ = curve_fit(law, C, Lb, p0=p, bounds=bounds, maxfev=20000)
    except RuntimeError:
        continue
    preds.append(law(target, *pb))
lo, hi = np.percentile(preds, [5, 95])
print(f"floor fit      L(1e23) = {law(target, *p):.3f}  90% band [{lo:.3f}, {hi:.3f}]")
print(f"pure power law L(1e23) = {10 ** (icpt + slope * np.log10(target)):.3f}")
print(f"truth (known only because this is a toy) = {true(target):.3f}")

3. Majority vote and best-of-N on a real model

Sample 16 answers per GSM8K problem, then compute maj@k and best-of-k with an oracle verifier (the gold answer). Best-of-k with an oracle is exactly pass@k, so we reuse the unbiased estimator from Module 13.


import math, random, re
from collections import Counter
from datasets import load_dataset
from vllm import LLM, SamplingParams

N = 16
ds = load_dataset("openai/gsm8k", "main", split="test").select(range(200))
gold = [float(ex["answer"].split("####")[-1].strip().replace(",", "")) for ex in ds]

llm = LLM(model="Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct")
tok = llm.get_tokenizer()
prompts = [tok.apply_chat_template(
    [{"role": "user", "content": ex["question"] + "\nThink step by step, then end with '#### <number>'."}],
    tokenize=False, add_generation_prompt=True) for ex in ds]
outs = llm.generate(prompts, SamplingParams(n=N, temperature=0.8, top_p=0.95, max_tokens=512, seed=0))

def extract(text):
    m = re.findall(r"####\s*\$?(-?[\d,]*\.?\d+)", text) or re.findall(r"-?[\d,]*\.?\d+", text)
    try:
        return float(m[-1].replace(",", "")) if m else None
    except ValueError:
        return None

answers = [[extract(c.text) for c in o.outputs] for o in outs]

def maj_at_k(ans, g, k, trials=50):
    hits = 0.0
    for _ in range(trials):
        sub = [a for a in random.sample(ans, k) if a is not None]
        if sub:
            counts = Counter(sub).most_common()
            tied = [a for a, c in counts if c == counts[0][1]]
            hits += random.choice(tied) == g
    return hits / trials

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):                 # unbiased estimator (Chen et al., 2021)
    return 1.0 if n - c < k else 1 - math.comb(n - c, k) / math.comb(n, k)

random.seed(0)
p_hat = [sum(a == g for a in ans) / N for ans, g in zip(answers, gold)]
for k in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16):
    maj = sum(maj_at_k(a, g, k) for a, g in zip(answers, gold)) / len(gold)
    bon = sum(pass_at_k(N, round(p * N), k) for p in p_hat) / len(gold)
    print(f"k={k:2d}  maj@k={maj:.3f}  best-of-k (oracle)={bon:.3f}")

# Theory check: binary-answer majority vote should HURT problems with p < 0.5
hard = [i for i, p in enumerate(p_hat) if 0 < p < 0.5]
maj16 = sum(maj_at_k(answers[i], gold[i], 16, trials=1) for i in hard) / max(1, len(hard))
mean_p = sum(p_hat[i] for i in hard) / max(1, len(hard))
print(f"{len(hard)} problems with 0 < p < 0.5: mean p={mean_p:.3f}, maj@16={maj16:.3f}")
Lab exit criteria

You're done when you have: (1) a table of maj@k and best-of-k for k = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 on 200 GSM8K problems, with a sentence explaining why best-of-k grows faster and what it would take to get it in production (a verifier); (2) the "hard problems" line, and an explanation of why maj@16 on them is above the binary-vote prediction (wrong answers scatter across different numbers, so the correct one can still win a plurality); (3) the scaling fit printout, plus how much the extrapolated loss moves when you drop the largest run; (4) three failure modes of your quality filter, each backed by a document you actually read.

Epoch AI estimates frontier training compute grows about 4× per year. What doubling time is that?

  1. About 3 months
  2. About 4 months
  3. About 6 months
  4. About 12 months
\(T_2 = \ln 2/\ln 4 = 0.693/1.386 = 0.5\) years. At 5× per year it's \(0.693/1.609 \approx 0.43\) years, about 5.2 months. "4× per year" is two doublings per year, not four.

A model solves a problem on 20% of independent samples. You draw 10 samples and have a perfect verifier. What's the chance you end up with a correct answer?

  1. 20%
  2. 50%
  3. 74%
  4. 89%
\(1 - 0.8^{10} = 1 - 0.107 \approx 0.89\). You fail only if all ten fail. 74% would be six samples (\(1 - 0.8^6\)).

On a yes/no question, a model is right on 40% of independent samples. What happens to majority-vote accuracy as you go from 1 to 101 samples?

  1. It falls toward 0%
  2. It stays at about 40%
  3. It rises toward 100%
  4. It converges to 50%
With binary answers and \(p \lt 0.5\), the wrong answer is the expected majority, and the binomial tail \(P(\text{more than half right})\) shrinks toward 0 as \(N\) grows. Voting amplifies whatever the model usually does. With free-form answers the picture differs, because wrong answers can split their votes.

Across model sizes, per-token accuracy on answer tokens rises smoothly from 0.80 to 0.95. Exact match on 20-token answers goes from about 1% to about 36%, which looks like a sudden new ability. What's the most likely explanation?

  1. The larger models acquired a new circuit at a threshold
  2. The all-or-nothing metric turns a smooth per-token gain into a sharp-looking jump, since \(p^{20}\) moves from 0.012 to 0.36
  3. The benchmark must be contaminated
  4. The pretraining loss curve must have a kink at that scale
This is Schaeffer et al.'s (2023) argument. \(0.8^{20} \approx 0.012\) and \(0.95^{20} \approx 0.36\). Re-scoring with a continuous metric would show steady improvement. That doesn't prove no ability ever emerges, but you should rule out this effect first.

You have 400B unique tokens of good code and want code to be 20% of a 15T-token pretraining run. What's the honest assessment?

  1. 0.75 epochs, no repetition at all
  2. About 2 epochs, comfortably safe
  3. About 7.5 epochs, past the ~4-epoch range where repetition is nearly free, so expect diminishing returns: lower the weight or find more code
  4. About 37 epochs, the model will memorize everything
\(e = 0.2 \times 15\text{T} / 0.4\text{T} = 7.5\). Using Muennighoff et al.'s fit, \(R = 6.5\) gives \(D' \approx 0.4\text{T} \times (1 + 15.4(1 - e^{-6.5/15.4})) \approx 2.5\text{T}\) effective, about 84% of the nominal 3T. It's not a disaster, but it's worse than spending those tokens on fresh data.

Why does training each generation only on the previous generation's samples lose the tails of the distribution first?

  1. Synthetic text has more grammatical errors than human text
  2. Models refuse to generate rare content
  3. Floating-point precision errors accumulate across generations
  4. Rare events are often missing from a finite sample, so each refit assigns them less probability, and once they're gone nothing brings them back
This is the statistical-approximation error in Shumailov et al. (2024). With a Gaussian, the MLE variance shrinks by \((n-1)/n\) in expectation each generation; with a long-tailed categorical, any category with zero count drops to zero probability for good. Keeping real data in every generation's mix anchors the distribution.

Ho et al. (2024) estimate that the compute needed to reach a fixed language-model performance halves about every 8 months. Over 4 years, roughly how much less compute does the central estimate imply?

  1. About 6×
  2. About 64×
  3. About 16×
  4. About 4,096×
48 months / 8 = 6 halvings, so \(2^6 = 64\times\). The 95% interval (about 5–14 months) gives roughly 11× to 780×, which is why the estimate should always be quoted with its range.
