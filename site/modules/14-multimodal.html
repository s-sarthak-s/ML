Module 14 · Beyond text

Seeing & hearing: how models read pixels and sound

A transformer only ever sees a sequence of vectors. To show it a photo, you cut the photo into 14-pixel squares and turn each square into a vector. To let it hear, you turn 30 seconds of speech into 3,000 thin slices of a spectrogram. This module walks through both pipelines number by number, from raw bytes to tokens, and shows where each conversion throws information away.

  6. Predict where multimodal models fail (small text, counting, spatial relations, long video) and design a test that exposes it.

Everything becomes a sequence of vectors

Module 05 turned text into token IDs, and each ID into a row of an embedding table. Module 06 pushed those rows through a stack of attention and MLP blocks. Nothing inside a block knows the vectors came from text. Attention mixes whatever vectors it's given, and the MLP transforms each one. So the recipe for every other kind of input is the same three steps:

  1. Chunk the signal into pieces: square patches of an image, 10 ms slices of audio, a handful of frames of video.
  2. Embed each piece as a vector of the model's width \(D\), usually with a small learned network (a linear layer, a few convolutions, or a whole pretrained encoder).
  3. Add position so the model knows where each piece came from: row and column for an image, time for audio, both for video.

After that, a picture is just a few hundred extra tokens in the context window. Most of this module is about the first two steps, because that's where the engineering choices live and where the information gets lost.

Definition · continuous vs discrete inputs

There are two ways to hand a non-text signal to a transformer. Continuous embeddings (ViT patches, Whisper frames) go straight into the residual stream as vectors. The model can read them but can't produce them, because its output head is a softmax over a vocabulary. Discrete tokens come from a learned codebook (VQ image tokenizers, audio codecs). They get IDs like words do, so the same model can both read and generate them. Most image and speech understanding models use the first kind; models that speak or draw need the second.

Intuition

A token is whatever the model attends over. For text, that's a word piece. For an image, it's a little square of pixels. The model's cost scales with how many of those you send, not with what they mean, so every modality comes down to the same bargain: fewer tokens are cheaper, and more tokens keep more detail.

What an image is to a computer

A digital image is a grid of pixels. Each pixel stores three brightness values, red, green and blue, usually as one byte each (0–255). Stack the three colour planes and you get a 3-D array, a tensor, of shape height × width × channels. That array is the entire image. There's no "cat" in the file, only 150,528 numbers for a 224×224 photo.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{X}} \in \{0,\dots,255\}^{\htmlClass{t2}{H} \times \htmlClass{t3}{W} \times \htmlClass{t4}{C}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t5}{\tilde{x}} = \frac{x/255 - \htmlClass{t6}{\mu_c}}{\htmlClass{t7}{\sigma_c}}$$
\(\mathbf{X}\)
the image: one integer per pixel per channel
\(H\)
height in pixels (number of rows)
\(W\)
width in pixels (number of columns)
\(C\)
channels: 3 for RGB, 1 for grayscale, 4 if there's an alpha channel
\(\tilde{x}\)
the normalized value the network actually receives, roughly zero-mean, unit-variance
\(\mu_c\)
per-channel mean from the training set. ImageNet: (0.485, 0.456, 0.406). CLIP uses its own: (0.481, 0.458, 0.408)
\(\sigma_c\)
per-channel standard deviation. ImageNet: (0.229, 0.224, 0.225)

In wordsAn image is \(H \times W \times C\) bytes. Before a network sees it, each byte is scaled to [0, 1] and then shifted and stretched so each channel has roughly mean 0 and spread 1, the same treatment the network saw in training.

Why it mattersSize grows with the square of resolution. Doubling the side length means 4× the pixels, and as you'll see, 4× the tokens and up to 16× the attention cost.

Think first: how many numbers are in a 12-megapixel phone photo?
4032 × 3024 × 3 = 36,578,304 bytes, about 36.6 MB uncompressed (the JPEG on disk is maybe 3 MB because JPEG throws away detail the eye won't miss). No vision model looks at that directly. Every pipeline starts by resizing, cropping, or tiling down to something like 224–1,000 pixels a side. Remember that step: it explains a lot of the failures in the last section.
Pitfall · preprocessing mismatch

The model learned on one exact pipeline, and quiet deviations cost accuracy without raising an error. OpenCV's cv2.imread returns BGR, not RGB. PyTorch wants channels first, [B, C, H, W], while PIL and NumPy give [H, W, C]. CLIP, SigLIP and ImageNet models each use different mean/std values and resize rules. Always run inputs through the model's own processor (AutoProcessor in transformers) rather than writing your own.

Convolutions and CNNs, briefly

From AlexNet (Krizhevsky et al., 2012) to ResNet (He et al., 2015), computer vision ran on convolutional neural networks. You'll still meet convolutions everywhere: in the stem of Whisper, in the patch embedding of every ViT, and in the encoders of image and audio codecs. The idea is simple. Slide a small grid of weights, the kernel, over the image, and at each position take the dot product of the kernel with the pixels under it.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{Y}[i,j] = \htmlClass{t4}{b} + \sum_{m=-k}^{k}\;\sum_{n=-k}^{k} \htmlClass{t2}{K}[m,n]\;\htmlClass{t3}{X}[i+m,\; j+n]$$
\(Y[i,j]\)
output value ("feature map") at row \(i\), column \(j\)
\(K\)
the kernel: a \((2k+1)\times(2k+1)\) grid of learned weights. 3×3 means \(k=1\), nine weights
\(X\)
the input image or previous feature map, padded at the borders
\(b\)
a learned bias added everywhere

In wordsEach output pixel is a weighted sum of the 3×3 neighbourhood around the matching input pixel, and the same nine weights are used at every position. (Strictly this is cross-correlation; deep learning calls it convolution anyway.)

Why it mattersWeight sharing is enormously efficient. A 3×3 conv on a 32×32 image has 9 weights; a dense layer with the same input and output size has over a million. Real layers have many kernels and many channels, but the saving is the same in kind.

Two properties come for free with convolution, and they're called inductive biases because they bake an assumption about images into the architecture:

  • Locality. Each output depends only on a small neighbourhood. Stacking \(L\) layers of 3×3 kernels (stride 1) grows the receptive field to \((2L+1)\times(2L+1)\) pixels, so deep layers see large regions built from small ones.
  • Translation equivariance. Shift the input and the output shifts by the same amount: \(\text{conv}(\text{shift}(X)) = \text{shift}(\text{conv}(X))\). A cat detector learned in the top-left corner also works in the bottom-right. Pooling layers then make the final answer roughly invariant to position.

The widget's shift slider checks the second claim: the output moves with the input and matches everywhere except a thin seam where the wrapped-around image meets the zero padding.

Intuition

Inductive biases are prior knowledge. They let a CNN learn from fewer examples because it doesn't have to discover that nearby pixels matter more than distant ones. The flip side is that a model with fewer built-in assumptions can learn better ones if you give it enough data. That trade is exactly what the Vision Transformer made.

The Vision Transformer: images as patch tokens

Dosovitskiy et al. (2020), in a paper titled An Image is Worth 16x16 Words, asked what happens if you drop convolutions and feed an image to a plain transformer encoder. Attention over individual pixels is hopeless: a 224×224 image has 50,176 pixels, and attention cost grows with the square of sequence length. Their answer was to cut the image into non-overlapping \(P\times P\) patches and treat each patch as one token.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{N} = \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{H}\,\htmlClass{t3}{W}}{\htmlClass{t4}{P}^2}, \qquad \htmlClass{t5}{d_{\text{patch}}} = \htmlClass{t4}{P}^2 \cdot \htmlClass{t6}{C}$$
\(N\)
number of patch tokens, the sequence length the encoder sees
\(H\)
image height after resizing (must be a multiple of \(P\))
\(W\)
image width after resizing
\(P\)
patch side in pixels: 16 in the original ViT-B/16, 14 in CLIP ViT-L/14 and most VLM encoders
\(d_{\text{patch}}\)
numbers in one flattened patch
\(C\)
channels (3)

In wordsA 224×224 image with 16-pixel patches is a 14×14 grid: 196 tokens, each a vector of 16·16·3 = 768 raw numbers. At 336 pixels with 14-pixel patches, it's 24×24 = 576 tokens.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{z}_0} = \big[\,\htmlClass{t2}{\mathbf{x}_{\text{cls}}};\; \htmlClass{t3}{\mathbf{x}_p^1}\htmlClass{t4}{\mathbf{E}};\; \htmlClass{t3}{\mathbf{x}_p^2}\htmlClass{t4}{\mathbf{E}};\; \dots;\; \htmlClass{t3}{\mathbf{x}_p^N}\htmlClass{t4}{\mathbf{E}}\,\big] + \htmlClass{t5}{\mathbf{E}_{\text{pos}}}$$
\(\mathbf{z}_0\)
the input sequence to layer 1, shape \((N+1)\times D\)
\(\mathbf{x}_{\text{cls}}\)
a learned "class" vector prepended to the patches; its final state summarizes the image
\(\mathbf{x}_p^i\)
patch \(i\), flattened to length \(P^2 C\)
\(\mathbf{E}\)
patch projection, shape \(P^2C \times D\), shared by all patches. Equivalent to a conv with kernel \(P\) and stride \(P\)
\(\mathbf{E}_{\text{pos}}\)
learned position embeddings, one per slot, shape \((N+1)\times D\)

In wordsEvery patch goes through the same linear layer to become a \(D\)-dimensional vector (768 for ViT-B, 1,024 for ViT-L). Add "you are patch number \(i\)" information, put a summary slot in front, and you have something a text transformer would recognize.

Why it mattersPosition embeddings are learned for a fixed grid. Run the model at a new resolution and the grid changes, so ViT interpolates the 2-D position table to the new size. Many recent encoders use 2-D RoPE instead, which handles any grid natively.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\text{FLOPs}_{\text{attn}}} \approx 4\,\htmlClass{t2}{N}^2\,\htmlClass{t3}{D}\cdot \htmlClass{t4}{L}$$
\(\text{FLOPs}_{\text{attn}}\)
work for the score matrix \(QK^\top\) and the weighted sum with \(V\), per image
\(N\)
tokens per image (patches + CLS)
\(D\)
model width
\(L\)
number of layers

In wordsHalve the patch size, or double the image side, and you get 4× the tokens and 16× the attention cost. The projections and MLPs only grow 4×, linearly in \(N\).

Data hunger

Without convolution's built-in biases, ViT has to learn locality from data. The original paper found that trained on ImageNet alone (1.3M images), ViTs trailed comparable ResNets; pretrained on much larger sets (14M–300M images), they matched or beat them. Modern vision encoders get that scale from web image-text pairs (CLIP, SigLIP) or self-supervision (MAE, He et al., 2021; DINOv2, Oquab et al., 2023).

CLIP: one space for pictures and words

A ViT trained on ImageNet knows 1,000 labels. To talk about images in open language, you need image vectors and text vectors that live in the same space, so that a photo of a dog lands near the sentence "a photo of a dog". CLIP (Radford et al., 2021) built that space from about 400 million image–caption pairs collected from the web, using two encoders and one loss.

  • An image encoder (a ViT or a ResNet) maps each image to a vector \(\mathbf{u}_i\).
  • A text encoder (a transformer) maps each caption to a vector \(\mathbf{v}_j\).
  • Both are projected to the same width and L2-normalized, so their dot product is a cosine similarity.

For a batch of \(N\) pairs, compute all \(N\times N\) similarities. The \(N\) matching pairs sit on the diagonal and the \(N^2-N\) mismatches everywhere else. Training pushes the diagonal up and everything else down.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathcal{L}} = -\frac{1}{2\htmlClass{t2}{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t2}{N}} \left[ \log \frac{e^{\htmlClass{t3}{s_{ii}}/\htmlClass{t4}{\tau}}}{\sum_{j} e^{\htmlClass{t3}{s_{ij}}/\htmlClass{t4}{\tau}}} + \log \frac{e^{\htmlClass{t3}{s_{ii}}/\htmlClass{t4}{\tau}}}{\sum_{j} e^{\htmlClass{t3}{s_{ji}}/\htmlClass{t4}{\tau}}} \right], \qquad \htmlClass{t3}{s_{ij}} = \frac{\htmlClass{t5}{\mathbf{u}_i}^{\top}\htmlClass{t6}{\mathbf{v}_j}}{\lVert \htmlClass{t5}{\mathbf{u}_i}\rVert\,\lVert \htmlClass{t6}{\mathbf{v}_j}\rVert}$$
\(\mathcal{L}\)
the loss: average of image→text and text→image cross-entropy
\(N\)
batch size. Every other item in the batch is a negative example
\(s_{ij}\)
cosine similarity between image \(i\) and caption \(j\), in [−1, 1]
\(\tau\)
temperature, learned. CLIP initialized it at 0.07 and clipped \(1/\tau\) at 100
\(\mathbf{u}_i\)
image embedding
\(\mathbf{v}_j\)
text embedding

In wordsEach image should pick its own caption out of the batch, and each caption should pick its own image. Softmax over a row (or column) of similarities, take the log-probability of the right answer, and average.

Why it mattersCosine similarities live in [−1, 1]. Without dividing by a small \(\tau\), the softmax over values like 0.31 vs 0.12 would be nearly uniform and the gradient tiny. The learned temperature turns small cosine gaps into confident probabilities.

Zero-shot classification

Once the space exists, a classifier is just a list of sentences. Write one prompt per class, such as "a photo of a {label}.", embed each with the text encoder, and pick the class whose text vector is closest to the image vector. No labelled training images, no new weights.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\hat{y}} = \arg\max_{c}\;\; \htmlClass{t2}{\mathbf{u}}^{\top}\, \htmlClass{t3}{\mathbf{v}_c}, \qquad \htmlClass{t3}{\mathbf{v}_c} = \text{TextEnc}(\text{"a photo of a } \htmlClass{t4}{c}\text{."})$$
\(\hat{y}\)
predicted class
\(\mathbf{u}\)
normalized embedding of the image to classify
\(\mathbf{v}_c\)
normalized embedding of the prompt for class \(c\). These rows act as the classifier's weight matrix
\(c\)
any label you can write in words

In wordsThe text encoder generates the classifier weights from label names on the fly. Add a class by writing a new sentence.

Why it mattersCLIP's best model reached ImageNet accuracy comparable to a supervised ResNet-50 without using any of ImageNet's 1.28M labelled training images. The paper also found that prompt templates and averaging embeddings over many templates added a few points over bare label names.

Where CLIP-style embeddings are weak

A contrastive loss only needs enough of the caption to pick the right image out of the batch, and web batches rarely contain "a horse riding an astronaut" next to "an astronaut riding a horse". Yuksekgonul et al. (2023) showed that such models often behave like bags of words, insensitive to word order and relations. They can also be fooled by text in the image: Goh et al. (2021) showed a CLIP model labelling an apple with a handwritten "iPod" note as an iPod. SigLIP (Zhai et al., 2023) replaces the batch softmax with a per-pair sigmoid loss and is now a common encoder choice for VLMs.

MLE angle

CLIP-family embeddings are a workhorse beyond classification. You can use them for text-to-image search (an ANN index over image vectors, Module 11), near-duplicate detection, and data filtering: LAION-5B kept web pairs whose CLIP similarity cleared a threshold. They're cheap, a single ViT forward pass per image, and you can cache them.

Vision-language models: plugging an encoder into an LLM

CLIP can match images to text but can't write anything. A vision-language model (VLM) can answer questions about an image, read a chart, or describe a screenshot, because it attaches a vision encoder to a full LLM. The most common design is almost embarrassingly simple.

The LLaVA recipe

LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) takes a pretrained CLIP ViT, keeps the per-patch output vectors (from the second-to-last layer), passes each through a small projector, and drops the results into the LLM's input sequence as if they were word embeddings. The LLM is unchanged. It just sees 576 extra "words" it was taught to read.

Definition · projector

The small network that maps vision-encoder outputs into the LLM's embedding space. LLaVA-1 used a single linear layer; LLaVA-1.5 switched to a two-layer MLP with a GELU. Many current models also compress in the projector, merging neighbouring patches into one token before the LLM ever sees them.

Three ways to fuse

Input-level fusion

Cheap to build, costs context

Reuses any pretrained LLM. Visual tokens occupy the context window and the KV cache like text, so a high-resolution image can cost thousands of tokens of prefill.

Cross-attention

Keeps the LM intact

Text sequence length doesn't grow with image size, and the frozen LM's text skills are protected (Flamingo's gates start at zero). The trade is extra layers, and the image can only be read where cross-attention layers sit.

Early fusion

One model, any order, any direction

The model can emit image tokens as easily as words. Training from scratch on mixed data is expensive, and Chameleon's authors reported stability problems they had to fix with architecture changes such as QK-norm.

Image token budgets

For input-level fusion, the number that decides cost is how many visual tokens one image becomes. Fixed-resolution models squash every image to one square: LLaVA-1.5 at 336 pixels gives 576 tokens whether you send a thumbnail or a 12 MP scan. That's cheap but loses detail, so newer models increase the resolution and then compress the tokens:

  • Tiling ("dynamic high resolution"). Cut the image into encoder-sized crops, encode each, and add one downscaled thumbnail for global context. LLaVA-NeXT's AnyRes used up to four 336-pixel tiles plus the thumbnail, around 2,880 tokens. InternVL 1.5 uses 448-pixel tiles with a configurable maximum tile count.
  • Native resolution. Qwen2-VL (Wang et al., 2024) feeds the image at roughly its own aspect ratio and resolution, with 2-D rotary positions, so the token count follows the pixel count. A min_pixels/max_pixels setting caps it.
  • Token compression. Merge each 2×2 block of neighbouring patch vectors into one token: either concatenate them (pixel shuffle, or space-to-depth, giving a vector 4× wider) and project back down, or average-pool them. InternVL turns 1,024 patches per 448-pixel tile into 256 tokens this way. Qwen2-VL's 2×2 merge means each LLM token covers 28×28 pixels.
$$\htmlClass{t1}{T_{\text{vis}}} = \htmlClass{t2}{n_{\text{crops}}} \times \frac{(\htmlClass{t3}{R}/\htmlClass{t4}{P})^2}{\htmlClass{t5}{r}^2}$$
\(T_{\text{vis}}\)
visual tokens the LLM must prefill for one image
\(n_{\text{crops}}\)
1 for a fixed-resolution model; tiles + 1 thumbnail for tiling
\(R\)
encoder input side in pixels (224, 336, 448…)
\(P\)
patch size (14 or 16)
\(r\)
merge factor per side: 1 for none, 2 for a 2×2 pixel shuffle or pool (÷4)

In wordsLLaVA-1.5: 1 × (336/14)² / 1 = 576. InternVL with 6 tiles + thumbnail: 7 × (448/14)² / 4 = 7 × 256 = 1,792. A native-resolution model at 1,288×728 with 28-pixel merged tokens: 46 × 26 = 1,196.

Why it mattersVisual tokens are prompt tokens. They add prefill latency, KV cache, and (with hosted APIs) billing. API providers publish their own per-image formulas based on tiles; check the current docs rather than guessing.

How VLMs are trained

The LLaVA recipe trains in two stages, and most later models elaborate on it rather than replace it.

  1. Stage 1: align the projector. Freeze the vision encoder and the LLM. Train only the projector on image–caption pairs (about 558K for LLaVA-1.5), with the plain next-token loss on the caption. This teaches the projector to output vectors the LLM already knows how to read. It's cheap: the projector is a few tens of millions of parameters.
  2. Stage 2: visual instruction tuning. Unfreeze the LLM (the encoder often stays frozen in LLaVA, though many later models unfreeze it too) and fine-tune on conversations about images: questions and answers, multi-turn chat, reasoning. LLaVA bootstrapped this data by giving text-only GPT-4 the COCO captions and bounding boxes for an image and asking it to write conversations; LLaVA-1.5 mixed in academic VQA, OCR and region-grounding sets for about 665K examples. As in Module 10, the loss is computed only on the answer tokens.

Production-grade models add a large pretraining stage between the two, on billions of tokens of mixed data, and usually train the encoder as well. The data mix decides what the model is good at:

Interleaved documents

Web pages with images in place

Datasets such as MMC4 (Zhu et al., 2023) and OBELICS (Laurençon et al., 2023) keep images inside their surrounding text, which teaches multi-image reasoning and in-context learning.

OCR, documents, charts

Reading, not just recognizing

Rendered text, scanned PDFs, tables, charts and UI screenshots. This is where resolution matters most, and where synthetic data (render text, then ask about it) is cheap and effective.

Grounding

Boxes and points as text

Region captions and "where is X" questions with coordinates written as tokens. Qwen2-VL wrote boxes on a 0–1000 normalized grid; Qwen2.5-VL switched to absolute pixel coordinates. This is what lets a model point at a button in a screenshot.

Video and multi-image

Time and comparison

Captioned clips, temporal grounding ("when does the door open?"), and image pairs for comparison questions. Covered in the video section below.

What sound is to a computer

Sound is air pressure wobbling over time. A microphone turns it into a voltage, and an analog-to-digital converter measures that voltage at regular intervals. The result, a waveform, is a single long list of numbers: one per sample, per channel. Two settings define it:

  • Sample rate \(f_s\): measurements per second. CD audio is 44.1 kHz, most speech models use 16 kHz, telephone audio is 8 kHz.
  • Bit depth: how finely each measurement is quantized. 16-bit gives 65,536 levels and about 96 dB of dynamic range (roughly 6 dB per bit, the same rule as Module 09's SNR). Models convert to float32 in [−1, 1].

So 30 seconds of 16 kHz mono speech is 480,000 numbers, or 960 KB at 16 bits. That's about three times the numbers in a 224-pixel photo, for an amount of speech that transcribes to maybe 80 words.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{f_s} \gt 2\,\htmlClass{t2}{f_{\max}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t3}{f_{\text{alias}}} = \Big| \htmlClass{t4}{f} - \htmlClass{t1}{f_s}\cdot \text{round}\big(\htmlClass{t4}{f}/\htmlClass{t1}{f_s}\big) \Big|$$
\(f_s\)
sample rate in Hz
\(f_{\max}\)
highest frequency you need to capture. \(f_s/2\) is the Nyquist frequency
\(f_{\text{alias}}\)
the frequency a tone appears to have after sampling
\(f\)
the tone's true frequency

In wordsYou need more than two samples per cycle to pin down a frequency. Anything above \(f_s/2\) doesn't vanish: it folds back and impersonates a lower frequency. A 10 kHz tone sampled at 16 kHz shows up as \(|10 - 16| = 6\) kHz.

Why it matters16 kHz keeps everything up to 8 kHz, which covers the bulk of speech intelligibility (fricatives like "s" extend higher, so 8 kHz telephone audio makes "f" and "s" hard to tell apart). Resamplers apply a low-pass filter before downsampling so high frequencies are removed rather than aliased.

Pitfall · the wrong sample rate

Feed 44.1 kHz samples to a model that assumes 16 kHz and every sound plays 2.76× slower and lower than it should; the model hears garbage and may still confidently produce text. librosa.load(path, sr=16000), whisper.load_audio and the Hugging Face feature extractors resample for you. Raw soundfile.read does not.

From waveform to spectrogram

A waveform is a poor input for a model. The same vowel spoken twice gives waveforms that barely line up, because the phases differ, while the frequencies it contains are nearly identical. Our ears work in frequency too: the cochlea splits sound into bands. So nearly every speech model first converts audio into a picture of how much energy there is at each frequency over time.

The Fourier transform

$$\htmlClass{t1}{X[k]} = \sum_{n=0}^{\htmlClass{t2}{N}-1} \htmlClass{t3}{x[n]}\;\htmlClass{t6}{w[n]}\; e^{-i 2\pi \htmlClass{t4}{k} n / \htmlClass{t2}{N}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t5}{f_k} = \frac{\htmlClass{t4}{k}\, f_s}{\htmlClass{t2}{N}}$$
\(X[k]\)
a complex number; its magnitude \(|X[k]|\) says how much of frequency \(f_k\) is present
\(N\)
window length in samples (Whisper: 400 samples = 25 ms at 16 kHz)
\(x[n]\)
the audio samples inside the window
\(w[n]\)
a window function (Hann) that fades the edges to zero, reducing spectral leakage
\(k\)
frequency bin index, 0 to \(N/2\) for real signals
\(f_k\)
centre frequency of bin \(k\). Bins are \(f_s/N\) Hz apart

In wordsFor each test frequency, multiply the signal by a sine and cosine at that frequency and add up the result. If the signal contains that frequency, the products line up and the sum is large; otherwise they cancel. It's a dot product against a bank of sinusoids (Module 01), and the FFT computes all of them in \(O(N\log N)\).

The short-time Fourier transform

Speech changes every few tens of milliseconds, so one Fourier transform of the whole clip would blur all the sounds together. The STFT slides a short window along the signal, moving by a hop each time, and takes a DFT per position. Stack the magnitudes as columns and you have a spectrogram: time on the x-axis, frequency on the y-axis, energy as brightness.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{S[m,k]} = \Big|\sum_{n=0}^{\htmlClass{t2}{N}-1} x[n + m\htmlClass{t3}{H}]\; w[n]\; e^{-i2\pi kn/\htmlClass{t2}{N}}\Big|^2, \qquad \htmlClass{t4}{T_{\text{frames}}} \approx \frac{\htmlClass{t5}{L}}{\htmlClass{t3}{H}}$$
\(S[m,k]\)
power at frame \(m\), frequency bin \(k\)
\(N\)
window length: sets frequency resolution \(f_s/N\) and time blur \(N/f_s\)
\(H\)
hop: samples between frames (Whisper: 160 = 10 ms)
\(T_{\text{frames}}\)
number of spectrogram columns
\(L\)
signal length in samples

In words30 s at 16 kHz is 480,000 samples; with a 160-sample hop that's 3,000 frames, 100 per second. Windows overlap (400-sample windows every 160 samples) so no moment falls between frames.

Why it mattersYou can't have sharp time and sharp frequency at once. A long window resolves close pitches but smears fast events; a short one does the reverse. Speech systems settle on 20–30 ms windows, short enough to catch consonants and long enough to separate harmonics.

The mel scale and log compression

Humans hear pitch roughly logarithmically. The step from 100 to 200 Hz sounds like a big jump (an octave); 5,000 to 5,100 Hz is barely noticeable. The mel scale warps frequency to match. A mel filterbank of overlapping triangular filters, evenly spaced in mel, pools the linear FFT bins into 80 or 128 bands: many narrow bands at low frequencies and a few wide ones at high frequencies. Then take the log, because loudness perception is also roughly logarithmic and the log tames the enormous dynamic range.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{m} = 2595 \cdot \log_{10}\!\left(1 + \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{f}}{700}\right), \qquad \htmlClass{t3}{\text{LM}[t,b]} = \log\Big(\epsilon + \sum_{k} \htmlClass{t4}{F_{b,k}}\; S[t,k]\Big)$$
\(m\)
perceptual pitch in mels
\(f\)
frequency in Hz
\(\text{LM}[t,b]\)
log-mel value at frame \(t\), mel band \(b\): the actual model input
\(F_{b,k}\)
triangular filter \(b\)'s weight on FFT bin \(k\)

In wordsBelow about 1 kHz the mel scale is close to linear; above it, close to logarithmic. 100→200 Hz is about 133 mels apart; 5,000→5,100 Hz is about 20.

Why it mattersAn 80-band log-mel frame replaces 201 linear FFT bins (for a 400-sample window) with fewer numbers, weighted toward the frequencies that carry speech. This formula is the HTK convention; librosa's default (and Whisper's filters) use the Slaney variant, which is exactly linear below 1 kHz. Same idea, slightly different numbers.

Intuition

A log-mel spectrogram is a grayscale image with 80 rows and 100 columns per second. That's why audio models borrow so much from vision: you can run convolutions over it, or cut it into patches (the Audio Spectrogram Transformer, Gong et al., 2021, does exactly that).

Whisper: speech recognition as sequence-to-sequence

Whisper (Radford et al., 2022) is an encoder-decoder transformer, the original Vaswani et al. layout, trained on 680,000 hours of audio paired with transcripts collected from the internet. Its design is deliberately plain. What made it strong was the scale and diversity of that weakly supervised data, plus a token format that packs several tasks into one decoder.

The numbers line up like this, and you can check every one: 30 s × 16,000 samples/s = 480,000 samples; ÷ 160-sample hop = 3,000 frames; ÷ stride 2 = 1,500 encoder positions, each covering 20 ms of audio. Model widths range from 384 (tiny) to 1,280 (large). The decoder's context is 448 tokens, which is plenty for 30 s of speech.

TokenRole
<|startoftranscript|>Begins every output.
<|en|>, <|de|>, … (99 languages; 100 in large-v3)Spoken language. Predict it to do language ID, or force it to skip detection.
<|transcribe|> / <|translate|>Write in the source language, or translate to English (translation only goes into English).
<|notimestamps|> or <|0.00|> … <|30.00|>Timestamp tokens in 20 ms steps mark segment boundaries, so the same decoder produces time-aligned subtitles.
<|nospeech|>Predicted when the window has no speech; its probability is used to skip silent chunks.
<|endoftext|>Ends the window. Long audio is processed as consecutive 30 s windows, using the last timestamp to decide where the next one starts.
Known behaviours worth testing

Every input is padded to 30 s, so a 2-second voice command costs as much encoder compute as a 30-second one. Because the decoder is a language model, it can hallucinate: on silence, music or noise it sometimes emits fluent text that was never said, or repeats a phrase in a loop. Voice-activity detection (vad_filter=True in faster-whisper) and the no-speech probability help. large-v3-turbo (OpenAI, 2024) cuts the decoder from 32 layers to 4, which makes decoding much faster with a small accuracy cost.

Audio as discrete tokens: neural codecs

Whisper's encoder produces continuous vectors, which is fine for reading audio. To generate audio with a language model, you want a vocabulary: the model predicts a token ID, and something turns IDs back into sound. Neural audio codecs provide exactly that. SoundStream (Zeghidour et al., 2021) and EnCodec (Défossez et al., 2022) share a shape:

  1. A convolutional encoder downsamples the waveform into a short sequence of latent vectors. EnCodec's 24 kHz model has a total stride of 320, giving 75 latent frames per second.
  2. A residual vector quantizer replaces each latent with a few codebook indices.
  3. A convolutional decoder turns the quantized latents back into a waveform.

All three are trained together, with reconstruction losses plus adversarial and perceptual losses so the output sounds right rather than merely matching sample by sample.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{r}_0} = \htmlClass{t2}{\mathbf{z}}, \quad \htmlClass{t3}{c_q} = \arg\min_{c}\, \big\lVert \htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{r}_{q-1}} - \htmlClass{t4}{\mathbf{C}_q}[c] \big\rVert, \quad \htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{r}_q} = \htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{r}_{q-1}} - \htmlClass{t4}{\mathbf{C}_q}[\htmlClass{t3}{c_q}], \quad \htmlClass{t5}{\hat{\mathbf{z}}} = \sum_{q=1}^{\htmlClass{t6}{Q}} \htmlClass{t4}{\mathbf{C}_q}[\htmlClass{t3}{c_q}]$$
\(\mathbf{z}\)
the encoder's latent vector for one frame
\(\mathbf{r}_q\)
residual: what's still unexplained after \(q\) stages
\(\mathbf{C}_q\)
codebook for stage \(q\): \(K\) learned vectors (EnCodec: \(K=1024\))
\(c_q\)
the chosen index at stage \(q\): this is the token
\(\hat{\mathbf{z}}\)
reconstruction: the sum of the chosen codewords
\(Q\)
number of stages (codebooks) used

In wordsStage 1 picks the nearest codeword. Stage 2 quantizes the leftover error, stage 3 the error after that, and so on. Each stage refines the last, the way you'd give directions: "Europe, then France, then Paris, then the 7th arrondissement."

Why it mattersOne codebook with enough entries for high quality would need billions of entries. \(Q\) codebooks of \(K\) entries give \(K^Q\) combinations from only \(Q\cdot K\) stored vectors. And you can drop the later stages to lower the bitrate, which is how one EnCodec model serves 1.5 to 24 kbps.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\text{tokens/s}} = \htmlClass{t2}{f_{\text{frame}}} \times \htmlClass{t3}{Q}, \qquad \htmlClass{t4}{\text{bitrate}} = \htmlClass{t2}{f_{\text{frame}}} \times \htmlClass{t3}{Q} \times \log_2 \htmlClass{t5}{K}$$
tokens/s
codec tokens a language model has to predict per second of audio
\(f_{\text{frame}}\)
latent frames per second (sample rate ÷ encoder stride)
\(Q\)
codebooks used per frame
bitrate
bits per second of compressed audio
\(K\)
entries per codebook

In wordsEnCodec at 6 kbps: 75 frames/s × 8 codebooks = 600 tokens per second, × 10 bits = 6,000 bits/s. A minute of audio is 36,000 tokens. Kyutai's Mimi codec (Défossez et al., 2024) runs at 12.5 frames/s with 8 codebooks of 2,048 entries: 100 tokens/s and 1.1 kbps.

Why it mattersText is about 3–4 tokens per second of speech. Audio tokens are one to two orders of magnitude denser, which is why audio LMs care so much about low frame rates.

Modelling several codebooks per frame

With \(Q\) tokens per frame, flattening them into one long sequence multiplies the length by \(Q\). Models avoid that in different ways. VALL-E (Wang et al., 2023) predicts the first codebook autoregressively and the rest in parallel. MusicGen (Copet et al., 2023) uses a "delay pattern" that offsets codebook \(q\) by \(q\) steps so all streams are predicted at once. AudioLM (Borsos et al., 2022) separates coarse semantic tokens (k-means clusters of self-supervised speech features, which capture what is said) from acoustic codec tokens (which capture how it sounds) and generates them in stages.

Speech in, speech out

A voice assistant has to hear, think, and talk. There are two ways to build one.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{T_{\text{response}}} = \htmlClass{t2}{T_{\text{endpoint}}} + \htmlClass{t3}{T_{\text{ASR}}} + \htmlClass{t4}{T_{\text{LLM}}} + \htmlClass{t5}{T_{\text{TTS}}} + \htmlClass{t6}{T_{\text{net}}}$$
\(T_{\text{response}}\)
time from the user's last word to the first sound of the reply
\(T_{\text{endpoint}}\)
how long you wait in silence before deciding the user has finished
\(T_{\text{ASR}}\)
time to final transcript after end of speech
\(T_{\text{LLM}}\)
time to first token (TTFT, Module 08) plus enough tokens for the TTS to start
\(T_{\text{TTS}}\)
time to first audio chunk from the speech synthesizer
\(T_{\text{net}}\)
network round trips and audio buffering

In wordsThe user waits for the sum of every stage on the critical path. Streaming moves work off that path: transcribe while the user is still talking, and start speaking the first clause while the LLM is still writing the rest.

Why it mattersConversation is fast. Stivers et al. (2009), measuring ten languages, found that the typical gap between turns is around 200 ms. A cascade that waits for silence, transcribes, generates the full reply and then synthesizes it easily exceeds a second and a half.

Cascaded

Modular and inspectable

Swap in a better ASR or a cheaper LLM independently. Every hop is text, so you can log it, evaluate it, filter it, and reuse your text tooling (RAG, tools, guardrails). Most production voice agents still look like this, with heavy streaming.

Audio-native

Lower latency, richer signal

One model hears prosody, laughter and hesitation and can answer in kind. Harder to train (audio tokens are dense; text reasoning can degrade), harder to evaluate, and harder to steer. OpenAI has said GPT-4o processes audio natively but hasn't published the architecture.

Video: frames times tokens

A video is a stack of images plus an audio track. Models handle it the way you'd expect: sample some frames, turn each into visual tokens, and concatenate them in time order with timestamps or temporal position encodings. The problem is pure multiplication. Thirty frames a second at 576 tokens each is over a million tokens for one minute.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{T_{\text{video}}} = \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{d}\cdot\htmlClass{t3}{f_{\text{sample}}}}{\htmlClass{t4}{t_c}} \times \htmlClass{t5}{T_{\text{frame}}}$$
\(T_{\text{video}}\)
visual tokens for the whole clip
\(d\)
duration in seconds
\(f_{\text{sample}}\)
frames sampled per second (typically 0.5–2, not 30)
\(t_c\)
temporal compression: frames merged into one set of tokens. Qwen2-VL merges 2 consecutive frames with a 3-D patch
\(T_{\text{frame}}\)
tokens per frame after spatial merging, e.g. 256 for a 448-pixel frame with 2×2 merge

In wordsA 10-minute video at 1 fps with 256 tokens per frame and no temporal merge is 600 × 256 = 153,600 tokens, already beyond a 128K context.

The tools for fitting video in are the image tools, applied harder: sample sparsely (0.5–2 fps, or pick scene-change keyframes), lower per-frame resolution, pool more aggressively (frames are spatially redundant and temporally redundant), and merge across time. Long-context models help: the Gemini 1.5 report (Gemini Team, 2024) demonstrated hour-scale video in context. Hosted APIs publish per-second token rates for video and audio, and those rates change between model versions.

Think first: what does sampling at 1 fps cost you?
Anything shorter than a second can fall between samples. A goal, a gesture, a two-second warning light, or a line of on-screen text that flashes by can be missing from the model's input entirely, and the model will answer from what it did see, often confidently. If your task depends on brief events, sample densely around them (or run a cheap detector first and only send the interesting segments).

Limits and failure modes, and how to test them

Most multimodal failures trace back to two causes covered above. Information was destroyed during conversion (downscaling, sparse sampling, bandwidth), or the language model's prior filled a gap with something plausible. Both are testable, cheaply, with synthetic inputs where you know the answer.

FailureWhy it happensCheap test
Small text, dense documentsA 4,032-pixel scan squashed to 336 pixels is 12× smaller; a 10-pixel-tall digit becomes under one pixel. Even at native resolution, each token covers 14–28 px.Render known text at sizes 6–40 px into an image; ask the model to transcribe it; plot accuracy against pixel height and against max_pixels.
CountingNothing in the architecture counts. Objects are spread across patch tokens and merged by pooling; answers drift toward common numbers.Generate images with 1–20 identical dots or shapes; compare model count to ground truth; look for the count where accuracy collapses.
Spatial relationsContrastive encoders are weak on word order and relations; "left of" depends on position embeddings surviving merges.Pairs of images with swapped object positions ("red cube left of blue sphere" vs right). Score the pair, not each image, so guessing gets 25%.
Hallucinated detailsThe LLM's prior supplies typical objects ("a kitchen" → "a fridge"). POPE (Li et al., 2023) measures this with yes/no questions about absent objects.Ask "Is there a X in the image?" for objects that are absent but likely in context; measure the false-yes rate.

MLE angle · build the probe set before you pick the model

Public benchmarks (MMMU, DocVQA, ChartQA, BLINK, and others) tell you roughly where a model stands. They won't tell you whether it reads your invoices at your scan resolution. Build 50–200 synthetic and real probes for the failure modes your product cares about, score them automatically (Module 13), and rerun them whenever you change the model, the processor settings, or the image pipeline. Resolution settings in particular are a quality knob and a cost knob at the same time.

1. CLIP zero-shot classification


import torch
from PIL import Image
from transformers import CLIPModel, CLIPProcessor

model = CLIPModel.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32").eval()
processor = CLIPProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/clip-vit-base-patch32")

image = Image.open("photo.jpg").convert("RGB")
labels = ["dog", "cat", "bicycle", "bowl of ramen", "city skyline"]
prompts = [f"a photo of a {l}." for l in labels]

inputs = processor(text=prompts, images=image, return_tensors="pt", padding=True)
print(inputs["pixel_values"].shape)          # [1, 3, 224, 224]: resized, cropped, normalized

with torch.no_grad():
    out = model(**inputs)
probs = out.logits_per_image.softmax(dim=-1)[0]   # logits = cos_sim * exp(logit_scale)
for l, p in sorted(zip(labels, probs.tolist()), key=lambda t: -t[1]):
    print(f"{p:6.1%}  {l}")

# The same thing by hand: normalized embeddings and the learned temperature
with torch.no_grad():
    u = model.get_image_features(pixel_values=inputs["pixel_values"])
    v = model.get_text_features(input_ids=inputs["input_ids"], attention_mask=inputs["attention_mask"])
u = u / u.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
v = v / v.norm(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
print("raw cosine:", (u @ v.T)[0].tolist())       # small numbers, typically 0.1-0.35
print("1/tau:", model.logit_scale.exp().item())   # about 100

A ViT-B/16 receives a 224×224 image. How many patch tokens does it create (not counting [CLS])?

  1. 14
  2. 196
  3. 256
  4. 3,136
224 / 16 = 14 patches per side, and 14 × 14 = 196. With the [CLS] token the sequence is 197. 256 would be a 16×16 grid (e.g. 224 px with 14 px patches is 16×16 = 256); 3,136 would be 4-pixel patches.

Using \(m = 2595\log_{10}(1 + f/700)\): the step from 200 to 300 Hz and the step from 6,000 to 6,100 Hz are both 100 Hz. Roughly how much larger is the low step in mels?

  1. About the same
  2. About 2×
  3. About 7×
  4. About 60×
m(200) ≈ 283, m(300) ≈ 402, a step of about 119 mels. m(6000) ≈ 2,546, m(6100) ≈ 2,562, about 17 mels. 119 / 17 ≈ 7. That's why mel filterbanks spend most of their bands on low frequencies, where pitch differences are most audible.

Whisper gets a 30-second clip. How many positions does its encoder attend over?

  1. 1,500
  2. 3,000
  3. 480,000
  4. 750
30 s × 16,000 = 480,000 samples. A 160-sample (10 ms) hop gives 3,000 log-mel frames. The second convolution in the stem has stride 2, so the encoder sees 1,500 positions, each covering 20 ms.

Why can CLIP classify images into categories it never saw labelled examples of?

  1. It was secretly trained on ImageNet's labels
  2. It has a 1,000-way classification head that generalizes
  3. Its image encoder memorizes the label of every web image
  4. Classes are defined by text: each label's prompt is embedded by the text encoder and the image is matched to the nearest one
The contrastive loss puts images and captions in one space. At test time the text encoder turns "a photo of a {label}." into a vector for each class, and those vectors act as the classifier's weights. Any label you can write becomes a class, with no retraining.

A 5-minute video is sampled at 2 fps, each frame becomes 256 tokens, and pairs of consecutive frames are merged (temporal compression 2). How many visual tokens?

  1. 38,400
  2. 76,800
  3. 153,600
  4. 600
300 s × 2 fps = 600 frames. Merging pairs gives 300 frame groups. 300 × 256 = 76,800 tokens. Without the temporal merge it would be 153,600, beyond a 128K window.

You record a 10 kHz whistle at a 16 kHz sample rate with no anti-aliasing filter. What does a spectrogram of the recording show?

  1. Nothing: frequencies above Nyquist are removed automatically
  2. A 10 kHz tone
  3. A 6 kHz tone
  4. An 8 kHz tone
The Nyquist frequency is 8 kHz. A 10 kHz tone folds back to |10 − 16| = 6 kHz. It doesn't disappear and can't be told apart from a real 6 kHz tone, which is why resampling must low-pass filter first.

A VLM with a fixed 336-pixel input reads a 4032×3024 photo of a receipt and makes up several prices. What's the most likely root cause?

  1. The photo was shrunk about 12×, so each 14-pixel patch covers roughly 170 original pixels and the digits are a pixel or two tall; the LLM filled in plausible numbers
  2. The tokenizer can't represent digits
  3. Sampling temperature was too high
  4. The vision encoder was never trained on any text
4032 / 336 = 12. Printed digits maybe 20–30 px tall become 2–3 px, under one patch. The information is gone before the LLM sees it, and its language prior writes something receipt-shaped. Fix it with tiling or native-resolution processing, or by cropping the region first, and test with rendered text at known sizes.
