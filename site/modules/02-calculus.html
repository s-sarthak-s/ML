Module 02 · Foundations

Gradients: how a model learns to walk downhill

Training a 7B-parameter model means nudging seven billion numbers, a few hundred thousand times, each one a little in the direction that lowers the loss. This module builds that loop from the ground up: derivatives, the gradient, the chain rule, and the optimizers (SGD, momentum, Adam, AdamW) that every training script calls in one line.

~2.5 hoursprereqs: 01hands-on: PyTorch, NumPy

By the end you can

  1. Read a derivative as "how far the output moves per unit nudge of the input", and compute it for the activation functions networks actually use.
  2. Compute a gradient by hand and explain why stepping along \(-\nabla \mathcal{L}\) is the fastest way down.
  3. Push derivatives through a computational graph with the chain rule, which is all backprop is.
  4. Predict from the learning rate and the curvature whether gradient descent will converge, zigzag, or blow up.
  5. Write Adam/AdamW from memory with every symbol explained, and wire up warmup + cosine decay + gradient clipping in PyTorch.

What training actually minimizes

Strip away the GPUs and the data pipelines and training is one sentence: find the parameter values that make a single number, the loss, as small as possible. The parameters are all the weights in the model, flattened into one very long vector \(\theta\). The loss measures how wrong the model's predictions are on the training data.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathcal{L}}(\htmlClass{t2}{\theta}) = \frac{1}{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \htmlClass{t4}{\ell}\big(f(\htmlClass{t5}{x_i};\, \htmlClass{t2}{\theta}),\; \htmlClass{t6}{y_i}\big)$$
\(\mathcal{L}\)
the loss: one scalar summarizing how wrong the model is on average
\(\theta\)
every trainable parameter, viewed as one vector (7 billion entries for a 7B model)
\(N\)
number of training examples. For an LLM, each next-token prediction counts as one
\(\ell\)
per-example loss, e.g. cross-entropy of the correct next token (Module 03)
\(x_i\)
the \(i\)-th input (a context window of tokens)
\(y_i\)
its target (the token that actually came next)

In wordsRun the model on every example, score how wrong each prediction was, and average the scores. Training means turning the knobs \(\theta\) until that average is small.

Why it mattersThere's one output and billions of inputs. You can't try knob settings at random. You need, for every knob at once, the answer to "which way should I turn you, and how much does it matter?" That answer is the gradient, and one backward pass computes all of it for roughly twice the cost of a forward pass.

Intuition

You're standing on a foggy hillside at night and want to reach the valley floor. You can't see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your boots. So you take a step in the steepest downhill direction, feel again, and step again. The first half of this module is about measuring the slope (derivatives, gradients, chain rule). The second half is about how to step: how far, with how much memory of past steps, and with what per-direction adjustments.

Derivatives: slope and sensitivity

Start with one input and one output. The derivative \(f'(x)\) answers: if I nudge \(x\) a tiny bit, how much does \(f(x)\) move, per unit of nudge? Geometrically it's the slope of the tangent line at \(x\). Practically it's a sensitivity: a derivative of 3 means the output moves about three times as far as the input, in the same direction.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{f'(x)} = \lim_{\htmlClass{t2}{h} \to 0} \frac{\htmlClass{t3}{f(x + h) - f(x)}}{\htmlClass{t2}{h}}$$
\(f'(x)\)
the derivative of \(f\) at the point \(x\), also written \(\frac{df}{dx}\)
\(h\)
the size of the nudge to the input; we take it toward zero
\(f(x+h)-f(x)\)
how much the output changed because of the nudge

In wordsRise over run, with the run shrunk until the curve looks straight. The ratio before taking the limit is the slope of a secant line through two nearby points; in the limit it becomes the tangent.

Why it mattersThe un-limited version is also your debugging tool. To check a hand-written backward pass, compare it against \((f(x+h)-f(x-h))/2h\) with \(h \approx 10^{-5}\) in float64. That's a gradient check.

The reason the derivative is useful for optimization is the next formula. Near any point, a smooth function looks like a straight line, and the derivative is that line's slope:

$$f(x + \htmlClass{t2}{\Delta x}) \;\approx\; f(x) + \htmlClass{t1}{f'(x)}\, \htmlClass{t2}{\Delta x}$$
\(f'(x)\)
local slope: the exchange rate between input change and output change
\(\Delta x\)
a small step in the input. The approximation gets worse as the step grows

In wordsFor small steps, output change ≈ slope × step. If you want the output to go down, step against the sign of the slope: \(\Delta x = -\eta f'(x)\) gives a change of \(-\eta f'(x)^2\), which is never positive.

Why it mattersThat one line is the whole justification for gradient descent. Everything about learning rates is about how small "small" has to be before this approximation stops being trustworthy.

You'll only ever need a handful of derivatives to read ML code. Here they are, with where each one shows up:

FunctionDerivativeWhere you meet it
\(x^2\)\(2x\)Squared-error loss; the \(2e\) at the end of every regression backward pass
\(e^x\)\(e^x\)Softmax and attention weights
\(\log x\)\(1/x\)Cross-entropy and log-probs. Small probabilities produce large gradients
\(\mathrm{ReLU}(x)=\max(0,x)\)1 if \(x\gt 0\), 0 if \(x \lt 0\)MLP activations. Undefined at exactly 0; PyTorch uses 0 there
\(\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}\)\(\sigma(x)\,(1-\sigma(x))\), at most 0.25Gates (LSTM, SwiGLU's SiLU = \(x\sigma(x)\)), logistic loss
\(\tanh x\)\(1-\tanh^2 x\), at most 1Older RNNs, the tanh approximation of GELU
\(\sin x\)\(\cos x\)Rotary position embeddings (Module 06)
Pitfall · finite differences in float32

Shrinking \(h\) doesn't keep helping on a computer. With \(h = 10^{-8}\) in float32, \(x+h\) rounds back to \(x\) and the quotient is 0 or garbage, because float32 only carries about 7 significant digits. The truncation error falls with \(h\) while the rounding error grows like \(\varepsilon/h\). For gradient checks use float64, the central difference, and \(h\) around \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-6}\).

Partial derivatives and the gradient

A loss depends on many parameters. A partial derivative \(\partial f / \partial \theta_j\) is the ordinary derivative with respect to one parameter while every other parameter is held fixed. Take

$$f(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 + w_1 w_2 + 3 w_2^2.$$

Treat \(w_2\) as a constant and differentiate in \(w_1\): \(\partial f/\partial w_1 = 2w_1 + w_2\). Treat \(w_1\) as a constant: \(\partial f/\partial w_2 = w_1 + 6 w_2\). At the point \((1, 1)\) these are 3 and 7: nudging \(w_2\) moves the loss more than twice as much as nudging \(w_1\).

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\nabla f}(\htmlClass{t2}{\theta}) = \left( \htmlClass{t3}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_1}},\; \htmlClass{t3}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_2}},\; \ldots,\; \htmlClass{t3}{\frac{\partial f}{\partial \theta_{\htmlClass{t4}{d}}}} \right)$$
\(\nabla f\)
the gradient ("nabla f"): a vector with one entry per parameter
\(\theta\)
the point where we evaluate it: the current parameter values
\(\partial f/\partial \theta_j\)
partial derivative: sensitivity of \(f\) to parameter \(j\) alone
\(d\)
number of parameters. The gradient has the same shape as \(\theta\)

In wordsMeasure each knob's sensitivity separately and stack the answers into a vector. In PyTorch this is literally p.grad, a tensor with exactly the same shape as the parameter p.

Why it mattersThe gradient points in the direction of steepest increase, and its length is the slope in that direction. So \(-\nabla f\) is steepest descent. The next card is why.

$$D_{\htmlClass{t1}{u}} f \;=\; \htmlClass{t2}{\nabla f} \cdot \htmlClass{t1}{u} \;=\; \lVert \htmlClass{t2}{\nabla f} \rVert \cos \htmlClass{t3}{\phi}$$
\(u\)
a direction to move in, as a unit vector (length 1)
\(\nabla f\)
the gradient at the current point
\(\phi\)
angle between the chosen direction and the gradient

In wordsThe slope in any direction is a dot product with the gradient (Module 01). It's largest when \(u\) points along the gradient (\(\cos\phi = 1\)), zero when \(u\) is perpendicular to it, and most negative when \(u\) points exactly opposite.

Why it mattersMoving perpendicular to the gradient keeps the loss constant, so the gradient is always perpendicular to the contour line through your point. The widget below shows it.

Caveat · steepest is not shortest

"Steepest descent" is a local statement about the current point. On a stretched-out bowl the steepest direction points mostly across the valley rather than along it toward the minimum. Drag the point in the widget to the end of a long ellipse and compare the green arrow with the direction to the centre. Most of the rest of this module deals with that mismatch.

The chain rule

Networks are compositions: a linear layer feeds an activation, which feeds another layer, then a softmax, then a loss. The chain rule says how sensitivities compose: rates multiply. If \(u\) moves 3 units per unit of \(x\), and \(y\) moves 2 units per unit of \(u\), then \(y\) moves 6 units per unit of \(x\).

$$\frac{d\htmlClass{t1}{y}}{d\htmlClass{t3}{x}} = \frac{d\htmlClass{t1}{y}}{d\htmlClass{t2}{u}} \cdot \frac{d\htmlClass{t2}{u}}{d\htmlClass{t3}{x}} \qquad \text{for } \htmlClass{t1}{y} = f(\htmlClass{t2}{u}),\;\; \htmlClass{t2}{u} = g(\htmlClass{t3}{x})$$
\(y\)
the final output (eventually: the loss)
\(u\)
an intermediate value computed along the way
\(x\)
the input (eventually: a weight)

In wordsSensitivity of the end to the start = (sensitivity of the end to the middle) × (sensitivity of the middle to the start). Example: \(y = \sin(3x^2)\). Let \(u = 3x^2\). Then \(dy/du = \cos u\) and \(du/dx = 6x\), so \(dy/dx = 6x\cos(3x^2)\).

When one value feeds into several places, each path contributes, and the contributions add:

$$\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t1}{L}}{\partial \htmlClass{t3}{x}} = \sum_{\htmlClass{t4}{k}} \frac{\partial \htmlClass{t1}{L}}{\partial \htmlClass{t2}{u_k}} \, \frac{\partial \htmlClass{t2}{u_k}}{\partial \htmlClass{t3}{x}}$$
\(L\)
the scalar at the end of the graph, usually the loss
\(u_k\)
every intermediate value that \(x\) feeds into directly
\(x\)
the variable we want the gradient for
\(k\)
runs over the outgoing edges of \(x\) in the graph

In wordsMultiply local derivatives along each path from \(x\) to \(L\), then add up the paths. A weight used in many places (a shared embedding, a weight applied at every sequence position) collects a gradient contribution from each use.

Why it mattersThis is exactly why PyTorch accumulates into .grad with += instead of overwriting it, and why you must zero gradients between steps.

Think first: stack 10 sigmoid layers. What happens to the gradient reaching the first layer?
Each layer contributes a factor \(\sigma'(z) \le 0.25\) to the product (times a weight). Ten of them give at most \(0.25^{10} \approx 9.5\times10^{-7}\), so the early layers barely learn. That's the vanishing gradient problem. ReLU's derivative is exactly 1 for positive inputs, which is a big reason it displaced sigmoid in hidden layers. Residual connections (Module 06) fix the rest: \(\frac{d}{dx}(x + F(x)) = 1 + F'(x)\), so there is always a path with factor 1.
MLE angle · why backprop runs backwards

You can multiply the chain of local derivatives starting from the input end (forward mode) or from the loss end (reverse mode). Forward mode gives the derivative of every output with respect to one input per pass. Reverse mode gives the derivative of one output with respect to every input per pass. Training has one output (the loss) and billions of inputs (the weights), so reverse mode wins by a factor of billions. The cost is memory: the backward pass needs the forward activations, which is why activation memory dominates training (Module 07) and why activation checkpointing trades recompute for memory.

Gradient descent

Now we can take steps. Gradient descent applies the linear approximation over and over: compute the gradient, step a short distance against it, repeat.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\theta_{t+1}} = \htmlClass{t2}{\theta_t} - \htmlClass{t3}{\eta}\, \htmlClass{t4}{\nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta_t)}$$
\(\theta_{t+1}\)
parameters after this step
\(\theta_t\)
parameters before this step (\(t\) counts steps)
\(\eta\)
learning rate ("eta"): how far to move per unit of gradient
\(\nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta_t)\)
gradient of the loss at the current parameters

In wordsMove every parameter a little in the direction that lowers the loss, by an amount proportional to how much that parameter matters right now.

Why it mattersFrom the linear approximation, \(\mathcal{L}(\theta - \eta g) \approx \mathcal{L}(\theta) - \eta\lVert g\rVert^2\), a guaranteed decrease if \(\eta\) is small enough. The whole question is: how small is small enough?

How big can the learning rate be?

The linear approximation ignores curvature. Near a minimum, any smooth loss looks like a bowl, and after rotating to the bowl's principal axes it becomes \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12 \sum_i \lambda_i \theta_i^2\). Each \(\lambda_i\) is the curvature along one axis, an eigenvalue of the Hessian (definition below). The gradient along axis \(i\) is \(\lambda_i\theta_i\), so one step of gradient descent does

$$\theta_i \;\leftarrow\; \theta_i - \eta\lambda_i\theta_i \;=\; (1 - \eta\lambda_i)\,\theta_i.$$

Every axis gets multiplied by its own factor \((1-\eta\lambda_i)\), every step. That single observation explains every behaviour you'll see in the widget.

$$\big|\,1 - \htmlClass{t1}{\eta}\,\htmlClass{t2}{\lambda_i}\,\big| \lt 1 \;\text{ for all } i \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0 \lt \htmlClass{t1}{\eta} \lt \frac{2}{\htmlClass{t3}{\lambda_{\max}}}$$
\(\eta\)
learning rate
\(\lambda_i\)
curvature along principal direction \(i\) (an eigenvalue of the Hessian)
\(\lambda_{\max}\)
the sharpest curvature anywhere; the direction most likely to blow up

In wordsIf \(\eta\lambda \lt 1\), that coordinate shrinks smoothly. If \(1 \lt \eta\lambda \lt 2\), it overshoots and flips sign every step but still shrinks: a zigzag. If \(\eta\lambda \gt 2\), it overshoots by more than it started and grows every step: divergence.

Why it mattersThe sharpest direction caps the learning rate, but the flattest direction sets how many steps you need. With \(\eta \approx 1/\lambda_{\max}\), the flat axis shrinks by \((1 - \lambda_{\min}/\lambda_{\max})\) per step, so you need on the order of \(\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\) steps. \(\kappa\) is the condition number. Momentum, Adam, and normalization layers are all, in part, attacks on a large \(\kappa\).

Definition · Hessian

The matrix of second derivatives, \(H_{jk} = \partial^2 \mathcal{L}/\partial\theta_j\partial\theta_k\). It describes curvature: how fast the gradient itself changes as you move. Its eigenvectors are the principal axes of the local bowl and its eigenvalues are the curvatures \(\lambda_i\). For \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12(\theta_1^2 + 10\theta_2^2)\) the Hessian is \(\mathrm{diag}(1, 10)\), so \(\lambda_{\max} = 10\) and the stable range is \(\eta \lt 0.2\). For a 7B model it's a \(7\mathrm{B}\times7\mathrm{B}\) matrix nobody ever forms; you only need to know it exists and that its top eigenvalue (the "sharpness") limits your learning rate.

Think first: on the quadratic, set η = 0.15. Which coordinate zigzags, and which crawls?
Along \(\theta_2\) (curvature 10) the factor is \(1 - 1.5 = -0.5\): it flips sign each step and halves, a fast zigzag. Along \(\theta_1\) (curvature 1) the factor is \(0.85\): no zigzag, steady shrinking. After the zigzag dies out in a few steps, the remaining time is spent crawling along \(\theta_1\). Raise η to 0.19 and the zigzag lasts much longer (factor −0.9) while the crawl speeds up (0.81). Past 0.2, \(\theta_2\) explodes.
Deep cut · the edge of stability

Real loss surfaces aren't fixed quadratics; the curvature changes as you train. Cohen et al. (2021) found that full-batch gradient descent on neural nets drives the sharpness \(\lambda_{\max}\) up until it reaches about \(2/\eta\), then hovers there while the loss keeps falling, non-monotonically. The Rosenbrock option shows a cousin of this: past a certain learning rate the iterate neither converges nor diverges but bounces across the valley indefinitely. Treat \(\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}\) as the local law, not a global guarantee.

Stochastic and minibatch gradients

The true gradient averages over all \(N\) training examples. For an LLM that's trillions of tokens, so computing it once per step is out of the question. Instead each step samples a minibatch and uses its average gradient as an estimate.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\hat{g}} = \frac{1}{\htmlClass{t2}{B}} \sum_{i \in \htmlClass{t3}{\mathcal{B}}} \nabla_{\theta}\, \htmlClass{t4}{\ell_i}(\theta), \qquad \mathbb{E}\big[\htmlClass{t1}{\hat{g}}\big] = \nabla\mathcal{L}(\theta)$$
\(\hat{g}\)
the gradient estimate actually used for this step
\(B\)
batch size: examples (or tokens) per step
\(\mathcal{B}\)
the randomly sampled set of example indices for this step
\(\ell_i\)
loss on example \(i\); its gradient is one noisy "vote" for the direction

In wordsPoll a random sample of examples instead of all of them. On average the poll points the right way (it's unbiased), but any single poll is off by some noise.

$$\operatorname{Std}\big[\htmlClass{t1}{\hat{g}}\big] = \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{\sigma}}{\sqrt{\htmlClass{t3}{B}}}$$
\(\hat{g}\)
minibatch gradient (each coordinate)
\(\sigma\)
spread of individual per-example gradients around the true mean
\(B\)
batch size, assuming independently sampled examples

In wordsAveraging \(B\) independent noisy votes cuts the noise by \(\sqrt{B}\), not by \(B\). Going from 32 to 512 (16× the compute per step) only cuts the noise 4×.

Why it mattersDoubling the batch eventually stops halving the number of steps you need. McCandlish et al. (2018) call the turning point the critical batch size; past it, extra batch is mostly wasted compute. LLM pretraining batches are measured in tokens: Llama 2 used about 4M tokens per step (Touvron et al., 2023).

Noise is not purely a cost. It's what lets cheap steps make progress, and there's some evidence that it steers training toward flatter regions that generalize better (Keskar et al., 2017, observed that very large batches tended to find sharper minima). It's also why the learning rate has to come down at the end of training: the size of the jitter cloud is proportional to \(\eta\), so lowering \(\eta\) shrinks it. That's half of the motivation for the schedules later in this module.

Pitfall · gradient accumulation and the mean

To fake batch 64 on a GPU that fits 16, run four micro-batches and call backward() on each before one optimizer.step(). Gradients add up across calls, so divide each micro-batch loss by 4 (the accumulation steps), or you've silently multiplied your learning rate by 4. With token-level losses and variable-length sequences, "the mean" has a further subtlety: averaging per micro-batch then across micro-batches weighs short and long sequences differently than one big batch would.

MLE angle · batch size and learning rate move together

For SGD, Goyal et al. (2017) popularized the linear scaling rule: multiply the batch by \(k\), multiply the learning rate by \(k\) (with warmup), up to a limit. For Adam-family optimizers the relationship is different, and a square-root rule has been proposed (Malladi et al., 2022). In practice, if you change the batch size of a known-good recipe, re-sweep the learning rate rather than trusting either rule.

Momentum

On an ill-conditioned bowl, plain gradient descent wastes most of its effort zigzagging across the narrow direction while it crawls along the long one. Momentum (Polyak, 1964, the "heavy ball") gives the iterate inertia: instead of stepping along the current gradient, step along a running sum of recent gradients.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{v_t} = \htmlClass{t2}{\mu}\, \htmlClass{t1}{v_{t-1}} + \htmlClass{t3}{g_t}, \qquad \theta_{t+1} = \theta_t - \htmlClass{t4}{\eta}\, \htmlClass{t1}{v_t}$$
\(v_t\)
"velocity": a decaying sum of past gradients, one entry per parameter, starting at 0
\(\mu\)
momentum coefficient, typically 0.9: how much of the old velocity survives each step
\(g_t\)
this step's (minibatch) gradient
\(\eta\)
learning rate

In wordsKeep pushing in the direction you've been going, and let the new gradient adjust it. Components that point the same way step after step pile up. Components that flip sign every step cancel.

Why it mattersWith a constant gradient \(g\), the velocity settles at \(g/(1-\mu)\), so \(\mu=0.9\) gives a 10× longer step along consistent directions. With a gradient that alternates \(+g, -g\) (the zigzag), the velocity settles at only about \(g/(1+\mu) \approx 0.53g\). Momentum boosts the long valley direction and damps the cross-valley one, exactly what ill-conditioning needs.

Convention check

Some texts write the velocity as an exponential moving average, \(v_t = \mu v_{t-1} + (1-\mu) g_t\). That version's velocity is \((1-\mu)\) times smaller, so the same trajectory needs a 10× larger \(\eta\) at \(\mu = 0.9\). PyTorch's torch.optim.SGD(momentum=0.9) uses the sum form above. Adam's first moment (next section) uses the average form. When you port a learning rate between codebases, check which one you're in.

Adam and AdamW

Momentum still uses one learning rate for every parameter. Parameters in a transformer have wildly different gradient scales: an embedding row for a rare token gets a gradient only when that token appears, a norm gain sees different magnitudes than a big attention matrix, and the scales drift during training. Adam (Kingma & Ba, 2015) gives every parameter its own step size by dividing its momentum by a running estimate of its typical gradient size. It's four lines, applied elementwise to every parameter:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{m_t} = \htmlClass{t2}{\beta_1}\, m_{t-1} + (1 - \htmlClass{t2}{\beta_1})\, \htmlClass{t3}{g_t}$$
\(m_t\)
first-moment estimate: an exponential moving average of the gradient. Starts at \(m_0 = 0\)
\(\beta_1\)
decay rate, default 0.9. It averages over roughly \(1/(1-\beta_1) = 10\) recent steps
\(g_t\)
this parameter's gradient at step \(t\)

In wordsA smoothed gradient: the direction this parameter has been pushed lately, with noise averaged out.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{v_t} = \htmlClass{t2}{\beta_2}\, v_{t-1} + (1 - \htmlClass{t2}{\beta_2})\, \htmlClass{t3}{g_t^{2}}$$
\(v_t\)
second-moment estimate: moving average of the squared gradient. \(\sqrt{v_t}\) is the typical gradient size (RMS). Starts at 0
\(\beta_2\)
decay rate, default 0.999 (memory ≈ 1000 steps). Many LLM recipes use 0.95 (memory ≈ 20 steps)
\(g_t^2\)
elementwise square of the gradient, so it's always positive and sign doesn't cancel

In wordsHow big this parameter's gradients usually are, regardless of direction.

Why it mattersGPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020) and Llama 2 (Touvron et al., 2023) both used \(\beta_2 = 0.95\). The commonly given reason: with a 1000-step memory, \(v\) reacts slowly when gradient scales jump, so a sudden large gradient meets a stale, too-small denominator and produces an outsized update. A shorter memory adapts faster and is less prone to that kind of instability.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\hat{m}_t} = \frac{m_t}{1 - \htmlClass{t3}{\beta_1^{\,t}}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t2}{\hat{v}_t} = \frac{v_t}{1 - \htmlClass{t4}{\beta_2^{\,t}}}$$
\(\hat{m}_t\)
bias-corrected first moment
\(\hat{v}_t\)
bias-corrected second moment
\(\beta_1^{\,t}\)
\(\beta_1\) raised to the power \(t\) (step count), not a superscript index
\(\beta_2^{\,t}\)
\(\beta_2\) to the power \(t\). With 0.999 it's still 0.37 at step 1000

In wordsBoth averages start at zero, so early on they're dragged toward zero. At step 1, \(m_1 = 0.1\,g_1\); dividing by \(1-0.9 = 0.1\) recovers exactly \(g_1\). Likewise \(\hat v_1 = g_1^2\). As \(t\) grows, \(\beta^t \to 0\) and the correction fades to 1.

Why it mattersWithout it, the first steps would use \(m_1/\sqrt{v_1} = 0.1g/(\sqrt{0.001}\,|g|) \approx 3.2\) instead of 1: about 3× too large, right when the model is most fragile.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\theta_{t+1}} = \theta_t - \htmlClass{t2}{\eta}\, \frac{\htmlClass{t3}{\hat{m}_t}}{\sqrt{\htmlClass{t4}{\hat{v}_t}} + \htmlClass{t5}{\epsilon}}$$
\(\theta_{t+1}\)
updated parameter
\(\eta\)
learning rate. Now roughly the largest change any parameter makes in one step
\(\hat{m}_t\)
smoothed direction (signed)
\(\hat{v}_t\)
typical squared size; its square root normalizes the step
\(\epsilon\)
small constant (PyTorch default \(10^{-8}\); Llama 2 used \(10^{-5}\)) that prevents division by zero and caps the step for parameters with tiny gradients

In wordsStep size = learning rate × (average gradient ÷ typical gradient size). When a parameter's gradients consistently agree, the ratio is near ±1 and it moves about \(\eta\) per step. When they're mostly noise that cancels, the ratio is near 0 and it barely moves.

Why it mattersThe units cancel: multiply every gradient by 1000 and Adam's update is unchanged (as long as \(\epsilon\) is negligible). That's why Adam learning rates like \(3\times10^{-4}\) transfer across models much better than SGD's, and why fp16 loss scaling doesn't distort Adam updates. If you set \(\beta_1 = \beta_2 = 0\), the update becomes \(\eta\,\mathrm{sign}(g)\): Adam is a smoothed cousin of sign descent, which is the core of the Lion optimizer (Chen et al., 2023).

AdamW: weight decay done right

Weight decay pulls weights toward zero to keep them from growing without bound. The textbook way adds \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^2\) to the loss, which adds \(\lambda\theta\) to the gradient. Inside plain SGD that's the same as shrinking the weights directly. Inside Adam it isn't: the extra \(\lambda\theta\) gets divided by \(\sqrt{\hat v}\) like everything else, so parameters with large gradients get almost no decay and parameters with small gradients get a lot. Loshchilov & Hutter (2019) proposed decoupling it:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\theta_{t+1}} = \theta_t - \htmlClass{t2}{\eta} \left( \frac{\hat{m}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{v}_t} + \epsilon} + \htmlClass{t3}{\lambda}\, \htmlClass{t4}{\theta_t} \right)$$
\(\theta_{t+1}\)
updated parameter
\(\eta\)
scheduled learning rate. In PyTorch it multiplies the decay term too
\(\lambda\)
weight decay coefficient. PyTorch default 0.01; LLM pretraining commonly uses 0.1
\(\theta_t\)
the current weight itself: every weight shrinks by the same fraction \(\eta\lambda\) per step

In wordsTake the normal Adam step, and separately shrink every weight by a fixed small fraction. The shrink bypasses the per-parameter normalization, so it treats all weights alike.

Why it mattersAdamW is the default optimizer for essentially all LLM pretraining and fine-tuning. torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=0.1) is the coupled L2 version, not AdamW. Biases and norm gains are usually excluded from decay.

What the race shows

On the axis-aligned bowl, Adam's per-coordinate scaling acts like a custom learning rate for each axis and it beats plain SGD easily. Rotate the same bowl 45° and the narrow direction no longer lines up with any single parameter. Adam's diagonal rescaling can't help as much, and its advantage shrinks. In real networks the bad curvature is partly axis-aligned (different layers and parameter types have different scales), which is a large part of why Adam works so well on transformers. Optimizers like Shampoo and Muon go further by preconditioning whole weight matrices; Muon has been used in some recent large pretraining runs (e.g. Moonshot AI's Kimi K2, 2025).

MLE angle · Adam's memory bill

Adam keeps \(m\) and \(v\) for every parameter, usually in fp32: 8 extra bytes per parameter. For a 7B model that's 56 GB of optimizer state before weights, gradients, or activations. Mixed-precision training with fp32 master weights lands around 16 bytes per parameter in total (Module 07). That bill is why ZeRO/FSDP shard optimizer state across GPUs, why 8-bit Adam exists, and why LoRA (Module 10) is so cheap: frozen weights need no optimizer state.

Learning-rate schedules: warmup and cosine decay

Almost no serious training run uses a constant learning rate. The near-universal recipe for transformers is a linear warmup from zero to a peak over the first few hundred or thousand steps, followed by a cosine decay down to a small floor (Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017, introduced cosine annealing).

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\eta_t} = \htmlClass{t2}{\eta_{\text{peak}}}\,\frac{\htmlClass{t6}{t}}{\htmlClass{t4}{T_w}} \;\;\text{if } \htmlClass{t6}{t} \lt \htmlClass{t4}{T_w}, \qquad \htmlClass{t1}{\eta_t} = \htmlClass{t3}{\eta_{\min}} + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\htmlClass{t2}{\eta_{\text{peak}}} - \htmlClass{t3}{\eta_{\min}}\big)\Big(1 + \cos\Big(\pi\,\frac{\htmlClass{t6}{t} - \htmlClass{t4}{T_w}}{\htmlClass{t5}{T} - \htmlClass{t4}{T_w}}\Big)\Big) \;\;\text{otherwise}$$
\(\eta_t\)
learning rate used at step \(t\)
\(\eta_{\text{peak}}\)
maximum learning rate, reached at the end of warmup (Llama 2 7B: \(3\times10^{-4}\))
\(\eta_{\min}\)
floor at the end of training, often 10% of peak in pretraining, 0 in many fine-tuning scripts
\(T_w\)
number of warmup steps (Llama 2: 2000; fine-tuning: often a few percent of the run)
\(T\)
total training steps
\(t\)
current step

In wordsRamp up in a straight line, then glide down along half a cosine wave: slow decrease at first, fastest in the middle, gentle again near the end.

Why it mattersThe decay half shrinks the minibatch-noise cloud from the previous sections, which is why loss curves often drop noticeably in the last stretch of a cosine schedule. The warmup half is about the first few hundred steps, explained below.

Why warmup helps

There's no single proven reason, but two explanations have solid support and both match the rest of this module.

  • At the start, Adam's statistics are unreliable. At step 5, \(\hat v\) is an average of five squared gradients. For some parameters it's badly underestimated, and dividing by a too-small \(\sqrt{\hat v}\) produces huge steps. Liu et al. (2020, RAdam) traced warmup's benefit largely to this variance problem.
  • The network also tends to start in a sharp region. At initialization the curvature can be high, so the full peak learning rate would violate \(\eta \lt 2/\lambda_{\max}\) and blow up. Small early steps let training move into flatter territory where a large \(\eta\) is stable (Gilmer et al., 2022, and Kalra & Barkeshli, 2024, study this). It's the edge-of-stability idea run in reverse.

Transformers with the original post-LayerNorm arrangement were notoriously dependent on warmup. Pre-LN (Xiong et al., 2020), now standard, is more forgiving but still uses it.

Pitfall · schedules count optimizer steps, not batches

With gradient accumulation of 8, 80,000 micro-batches is 10,000 optimizer steps. Pass the scheduler the number of optimizer.step() calls, and call scheduler.step() once per optimizer step, after optimizer.step(). Get this wrong and your cosine finishes an eighth of the way through training, or never decays at all. Also note that Hugging Face's get_cosine_schedule_with_warmup decays to exactly 0 and starts warmup at 0, so the very first update uses a learning rate of 0.

Gradient clipping and loss spikes

Every so often a batch produces a gradient tens or hundreds of times larger than normal: an odd document, a rare token, a numerical hiccup. One giant step can undo hours of training. The standard guard is to cap the gradient's overall length before the optimizer sees it.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{g} \;\leftarrow\; \htmlClass{t1}{g} \cdot \min\!\left(1,\; \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{c}}{\lVert \htmlClass{t1}{g} \rVert_2}\right), \qquad \lVert \htmlClass{t1}{g} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{\htmlClass{t3}{p}} \lVert g_{\htmlClass{t3}{p}} \rVert_2^2}$$
\(g\)
the full gradient: all parameter tensors' gradients viewed as one long vector
\(c\)
the threshold, max_norm. 1.0 is the common LLM value (GPT-3, Llama 2)
\(p\)
each parameter tensor. The norm is global, computed over all of them together

In wordsIf the whole gradient vector is longer than \(c\), shrink it to length exactly \(c\). If it's shorter, leave it alone. Every tensor gets the same scale factor, so the direction never changes.

Why it mattersIt bounds the damage from one bad batch while leaving normal steps untouched. The norm before clipping (what clip_grad_norm_ returns) is one of the most useful numbers to log during training.

Loss spikes

Large runs occasionally show a sudden jump in loss, sometimes recovering in a few hundred steps, sometimes diverging for good. Causes vary: a learning rate near the stability edge, bad data, attention logits that grow until softmax saturates, or Adam's \(\hat v\) lagging behind a sudden change in gradient scale. The remedies teams report, roughly in the order they reach for them:

  1. Make sure clipping is on, and watch the gradient-norm curve. Spikes in the norm usually precede spikes in the loss.
  2. Lower the peak learning rate, lengthen warmup, or drop \(\beta_2\) from 0.999 to 0.95.
  3. Restart from a checkpoint before the spike and skip the offending batches. The PaLM team did exactly this, rewinding about 100 steps and skipping roughly 200–500 batches (Chowdhery et al., 2022).
  4. Architectural stabilizers: QK-norm on attention (Dehghani et al., 2023) and an auxiliary z-loss on the softmax normalizer (used in PaLM).
MLE angle · what to log every step

Loss, learning rate, and pre-clip gradient norm, at minimum. If the gradient norm sits above max_norm most of the time, clipping has quietly become your learning-rate schedule. The fix is usually a lower learning rate, not a higher threshold. A slow upward drift in the norm over thousands of steps is an early warning worth investigating before it becomes a spike.

Saddle points, local minima, and high-dimensional loss surfaces

Where the gradient is zero, gradient descent stops. These critical points come in three kinds, told apart by the Hessian's eigenvalues, the curvatures along each principal direction:

Critical pointHessian eigenvaluesShapeProblem for training?
Local minimumall positivebowlOnly if its loss is much worse than other minima
Local maximumall negativedomeNo; any nudge rolls you off
Saddle pointmixed signsmountain passIt slows training: gradients are tiny nearby

Low-dimensional intuition says local minima are the danger. In high dimensions it's the reverse. For a point to be a minimum, the curvature must be positive in every one of millions of directions at once; if the signs behaved anything like independent coin flips, that would essentially never happen by chance. Dauphin et al. (2014) argued that most critical points with high loss in large networks are saddles, and that the plateaus seen in training are often the optimizer lingering near them. Noise from minibatches helps push it off.

What we believe about real loss surfaces

A few empirical findings to calibrate your mental picture, each from a different line of work. In large networks, the local minima that training actually finds tend to have similar loss to each other (Choromanska et al., 2015). Different solutions can often be joined by paths along which the loss stays low (Garipov et al., 2018; Draxler et al., 2018). Pretty 3D loss-surface renderings (Li et al., 2018) are 2D slices through a space with billions of dimensions, useful for comparisons and misleading as literal maps. And the Hessian of a trained network typically has a handful of large eigenvalues and a huge bulk near zero: most directions are almost flat.

1. Autograd does the chain rule for you

Reproduce the computational-graph figure in PyTorch. The printed gradients should match the green labels.


import torch

x, y = torch.tensor(2.0), torch.tensor(1.0)
w = torch.tensor(0.5, requires_grad=True)    # leaves we want gradients for
b = torch.tensor(-0.5, requires_grad=True)

a = torch.sigmoid(w * x + b)                 # forward pass records the graph
L = (a - y) ** 2
L.backward()                                 # reverse-mode chain rule
print(L.item(), w.grad, b.grad)              # 0.1425  tensor(-0.3549)  tensor(-0.1774)

# Gradients ACCUMULATE: run forward/backward again without zeroing...
L = (torch.sigmoid(w * x + b) - y) ** 2
L.backward()
print(w.grad)                                # tensor(-0.7098): doubled, the sum-over-uses rule
w.grad = None; b.grad = None                 # what optimizer.zero_grad() does

# Gradient check against a central finite difference, in float64
def loss_fn(w_):
    return (torch.sigmoid(w_ * x.double() + b.detach().double()) - y.double()) ** 2
w64 = torch.tensor(0.5, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
loss_fn(w64).backward()
h = 1e-6
fd = (loss_fn(torch.tensor(0.5 + h, dtype=torch.float64)) - loss_fn(torch.tensor(0.5 - h, dtype=torch.float64))) / (2 * h)
print(w64.grad.item(), fd.item())            # agree to ~1e-10

2. Adam in fifteen lines of NumPy

Write it yourself, then check it against torch.optim.AdamW step for step. If the two disagree, one of the four formulas is wrong.


import numpy as np

class AdamW:
    def __init__(self, shape, lr=1e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.999), eps=1e-8, weight_decay=0.0):
        self.m, self.v, self.t = np.zeros(shape), np.zeros(shape), 0
        self.lr, (self.b1, self.b2), self.eps, self.wd = lr, betas, eps, weight_decay

    def step(self, theta, g):
        self.t += 1
        self.m = self.b1 * self.m + (1 - self.b1) * g          # first moment
        self.v = self.b2 * self.v + (1 - self.b2) * g * g      # second moment
        m_hat = self.m / (1 - self.b1 ** self.t)               # bias correction
        v_hat = self.v / (1 - self.b2 ** self.t)
        theta = theta * (1 - self.lr * self.wd)                # decoupled weight decay
        return theta - self.lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + self.eps)

# --- verify against PyTorch on an ill-conditioned quadratic L = 0.5 * theta^T A theta
import torch
A = np.diag([1.0, 25.0])
theta = np.array([-3.0, 2.0])
mine = AdamW(2, lr=0.1, weight_decay=0.01)
p = torch.tensor(theta.copy(), requires_grad=True)             # float64, like NumPy
ref = torch.optim.AdamW([p], lr=0.1, weight_decay=0.01)
for _ in range(200):
    theta = mine.step(theta, A @ theta)                        # gradient of the quadratic is A theta
    ref.zero_grad()
    (0.5 * p @ torch.from_numpy(A) @ p).backward()
    ref.step()
print(theta, p.detach().numpy())                               # identical to ~1e-12

3. A real training loop: AdamW + warmup/cosine + clipping

The same skeleton appears in nanoGPT, the Hugging Face Trainer, and most fine-tuning scripts. This version is self-contained so you can run it on a laptop CPU and watch the logged learning rate and gradient norm.


import torch
from torch import nn
from transformers import get_cosine_schedule_with_warmup

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(32, 256), nn.GELU(), nn.LayerNorm(256), nn.Linear(256, 1))
w_true = torch.randn(32, 1) / 32 ** 0.5                       # keeps x @ w_true at unit scale

# No weight decay on biases and norm gains (1-D tensors); decay the matrices.
decay    = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.ndim >= 2]
no_decay = [p for p in model.parameters() if p.ndim < 2]
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(
    [{"params": decay, "weight_decay": 0.1}, {"params": no_decay, "weight_decay": 0.0}],
    lr=3e-3, betas=(0.9, 0.95), eps=1e-8,
)
total_steps, warmup_steps = 2000, 100
sched = get_cosine_schedule_with_warmup(opt, num_warmup_steps=warmup_steps, num_training_steps=total_steps)

for step in range(total_steps):
    xb = torch.randn(64, 32)                                   # a fresh minibatch, B = 64
    yb = torch.sin(xb @ w_true) + 0.1 * torch.randn(64, 1)
    loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(model(xb), yb)

    loss.backward()
    gnorm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)  # returns pre-clip norm
    opt.step()
    sched.step()                                               # once per optimizer step, after opt.step()
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)

    if step % 200 == 0 or step == total_steps - 1:
        print(f"step {step:4d}  loss {loss.item():.4f}  lr {sched.get_last_lr()[0]:.2e}  grad_norm {gnorm.item():.3f}")
Lab exit criteria

You're done when (1) your NumPy AdamW matches torch.optim.AdamW to within \(10^{-10}\) after 200 steps; (2) on the quadratic \(\tfrac12(\theta_1^2 + 10\theta_2^2)\), plain gradient descent converges at \(\eta = 0.19\) and diverges at \(\eta = 0.21\), and you can say why from the \(2/\lambda_{\max}\) bound; and (3) in the training loop you can point to the step where warmup ends in the logged LR, and you've re-run with max_norm=0.1 and explained what changed in the loss curve.

\(f(w_1, w_2) = w_1^2 w_2 + 3w_2\). What is \(\nabla f\) at \((w_1, w_2) = (1, 2)\)?

  1. \((2, 4)\)
  2. \((4, 1)\)
  3. \((4, 4)\)
  4. \((4, 7)\)
\(\partial f/\partial w_1 = 2w_1w_2 = 4\). \(\partial f/\partial w_2 = w_1^2 + 3 = 4\). Hold the other variable constant for each partial, then evaluate at the point.

Plain gradient descent on \(\mathcal{L} = \tfrac12(4\theta_1^2 + 100\theta_2^2)\) with \(\eta = 0.025\). What happens?

  1. Converges smoothly in both coordinates
  2. Zigzags along \(\theta_2\) but converges
  3. Converges along \(\theta_2\) but diverges along \(\theta_1\)
  4. Diverges along \(\theta_2\) while \(\theta_1\) converges
The curvatures are 4 and 100. Along \(\theta_2\): \(\eta\lambda = 2.5 \gt 2\), so the factor is \(1-2.5 = -1.5\) and \(\theta_2\) grows 1.5× (with a sign flip) every step. Along \(\theta_1\): \(\eta\lambda = 0.1\), factor 0.9, converges. The stable range is \(\eta \lt 2/100 = 0.02\).

Adam with default \(\beta_1 = 0.9,\ \beta_2 = 0.999\), \(\eta = 10^{-3}\). At step 1, a parameter's gradient is \(g_1 = 0.02\). How large is its first update (ignore \(\epsilon\))?

  1. About \(10^{-3}\), i.e. \(\eta\), whatever the size of \(g_1\)
  2. \(2\times10^{-5}\), i.e. \(\eta g_1\)
  3. About \(3.2\times10^{-3}\)
  4. \(2\times10^{-6}\)
\(m_1 = 0.1 \cdot 0.02 = 0.002\), so \(\hat m_1 = 0.002/0.1 = 0.02\). \(v_1 = 0.001 \cdot 0.0004\), so \(\hat v_1 = 0.0004\) and \(\sqrt{\hat v_1} = 0.02\). The ratio is exactly 1, and the update is \(\eta\). Option C is what you'd get without bias correction: \(0.002/\sqrt{4\times10^{-7}} \approx 3.16\).

\(L = (a-1)^2\), \(a = \sigma(z)\), \(z = 2w\). At the current \(w\), \(a = 0.8\). What is \(dL/dw\)?

  1. \(-0.4\)
  2. \(-0.128\)
  3. \(-0.064\)
  4. \(0.16\)
Multiply the local derivatives along the chain: \(\frac{dL}{da} = 2(a-1) = -0.4\), \(\frac{da}{dz} = a(1-a) = 0.16\), \(\frac{dz}{dw} = 2\). Product: \(-0.4 \times 0.16 \times 2 = -0.128\). Option C forgets the factor 2 from \(z = 2w\).

You raise the batch size from 32 to 512, keeping everything else fixed. By how much does the standard deviation of the minibatch gradient fall?

  1. 16×
  2. 2×
  3. 4×
  4. It doesn't; the noise comes from the data, not the batch
Noise scales as \(\sigma/\sqrt{B}\). The batch grew 16×, so the noise falls by \(\sqrt{16} = 4\). That square root is why bigger batches show diminishing returns past the critical batch size.

A model has two parameter tensors whose gradients have norms 6 and 8. You call clip_grad_norm_(params, max_norm=5). What are their norms afterwards?

  1. 5 and 5
  2. 3 and 4
  3. 5 and 8
  4. 2.5 and 2.5
The global norm is \(\sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10 \gt 5\), so every gradient is multiplied by \(5/10 = 0.5\), giving 3 and 4. The combined norm is now exactly 5 and the direction is unchanged. Option A is what per-tensor clipping would do, which changes the direction.

SGD with momentum (PyTorch convention), \(\mu = 0.9\), learning rate \(\eta\). The gradient stays at the same value \(g\) for many steps. What does each step's displacement approach?

  1. \(\eta g\)
  2. \(0.9\,\eta g\)
  3. \(1.9\,\eta g\)
  4. \(10\,\eta g\)
The velocity converges to the fixed point of \(v = 0.9v + g\), which is \(v = g/(1-0.9) = 10g\). Each step moves \(\eta v = 10\eta g\). That's the 10× acceleration along consistent directions; it's also why switching from plain SGD to momentum usually requires lowering \(\eta\).
