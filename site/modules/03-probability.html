Module 03 · Foundations

Probability & information: how a model keeps score

Every language model ends the same way. A softmax turns a vector of raw scores into a probability for each of ~100K tokens, and the training loss is minus the log of the probability it gave the token that actually came next. This module builds that pipeline from the ground up, one symbol at a time, and then uses it to read loss curves, perplexities, and KL penalties without guessing.

~2.5 hoursprereqs: 01, 02hands-on: PyTorch, transformers

By the end you can

  1. Write the probability of a sentence as a product of next-token probabilities, and explain why we add logs instead.
  2. Compute a softmax by hand, say what temperature does, and know why adding a constant to every logit changes nothing.
  3. Turn a model's probability for the right token into a cross-entropy loss and its gradient, in nats and in bits.
  4. Read entropy, KL divergence, and perplexity as numbers with units, and know which direction of KL a training recipe is using.
  5. Compute a Hugging Face model's perplexity on your own text, and say when two perplexities can't be compared.

Random variables and distributions

A random variable is a quantity whose value you don't know yet, together with a rule saying how likely each value is. That rule is its distribution. For a language model, the most important random variable is "the next token". Its possible values are the entries of the vocabulary: 50,257 for GPT-2, 128,256 for Llama 3. Each forward pass of the model produces one full distribution over all of them.

Definition · Categorical distribution

A random variable \(X\) that takes one of \(V\) discrete values has a categorical distribution, given by a vector \(\mathbf{p} = (p_1, \ldots, p_V)\) with \(P(X = k) = p_k\), every \(p_k \ge 0\), and \(\sum_{k=1}^{V} p_k = 1\). The output of an LLM's final softmax is exactly such a vector, one per position.

Some quantities are continuous instead: a weight value, an activation, a latency. These have a probability density \(p(x)\). A density is not a probability; it can exceed 1. Probabilities come from areas under it: \(P(a \le X \le b) = \int_a^b p(x)\,dx\), and the total area is 1.

The Gaussian

The most important density is the normal or Gaussian distribution, the bell curve. It shows up in ML for two reasons. Sums of many small independent effects tend toward it (the central limit theorem), and we choose it on purpose: GPT-2 initializes its weights from a Gaussian with standard deviation 0.02. Trained weight matrices stay roughly bell-shaped, though usually with heavier tails than a true Gaussian, which is the assumption NF4 quantization builds on (Module 09).

$$\htmlClass{t1}{p(x)} = \frac{1}{\htmlClass{t3}{\sigma}\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(\htmlClass{t4}{x} - \htmlClass{t2}{\mu})^2}{2\htmlClass{t3}{\sigma}^2}\right)$$
\(p(x)\)
density at value \(x\). Written \(\mathcal{N}(x;\mu,\sigma^2)\)
\(\mu\)
mean: where the peak sits
\(\sigma\)
standard deviation: how wide the bell is. \(\sigma^2\) is the variance
\(x\)
the value you're asking about

In wordsThe density falls off with the squared distance from the mean, measured in units of \(\sigma\). The \(\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\) in front is just whatever constant makes the total area 1, so a narrower bell has to be taller.

Why it mattersAbout 68.3% of the mass lies within \(\pm1\sigma\), 95.4% within \(\pm2\sigma\), 99.7% within \(\pm3\sigma\). If a weight sits at \(6\sigma\) you are looking at an outlier, not bad luck. A true Gaussian produces one about twice per billion draws.

Expectation and variance

Two numbers summarize most distributions well enough for engineering: where the center is, and how spread out the values are.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbb{E}[X]} = \sum_{x} \htmlClass{t2}{x}\;\htmlClass{t3}{P(X = x)}$$
\(\mathbb{E}[X]\)
expected value, often written \(\mu\)
\(x\)
each possible value
\(P(X=x)\)
its probability, which acts as the weight

In wordsA probability-weighted average. For a density, the sum becomes an integral: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \int x\,p(x)\,dx\). The same recipe works for any function of \(X\): \(\mathbb{E}[f(X)] = \sum_x f(x)\,P(X=x)\).

Why it mattersThe training loss is an expectation: the average of \(-\log p_\theta(\text{next token})\) over the data distribution. You can't compute it exactly, so every minibatch average is an estimate of it, and the noise in that estimate is the "stochastic" in stochastic gradient descent.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\operatorname{Var}[X]} = \mathbb{E}\big[(X - \htmlClass{t2}{\mu})^2\big] = \htmlClass{t3}{\mathbb{E}[X^2]} - \htmlClass{t2}{\mu}^2, \qquad \htmlClass{t4}{\sigma} = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}[X]}$$
\(\operatorname{Var}[X]\)
variance: average squared distance from the mean
\(\mu\)
the mean, \(\mathbb{E}[X]\)
\(\mathbb{E}[X^2]\)
mean of the squared values (the "second moment")
\(\sigma\)
standard deviation, in the same units as \(X\)

In wordsSquare the distances so positive and negative deviations don't cancel, then average. The second form is easier to compute: mean of the squares minus the square of the mean.

Worked example: tokens per word

Suppose a tokenizer splits English words into 1, 2 or 3 tokens with the probabilities below. These are illustrative, not measured from a real tokenizer.

Tokens per word \(x\)\(P(X=x)\)\(x \cdot P\)\(x^2 \cdot P\)
10.60.60.6
20.30.61.2
30.10.30.9
Sum1.0\(\mathbb{E}[X] = 1.5\)\(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 2.7\)

So \(\operatorname{Var}[X] = 2.7 - 1.5^2 = 0.45\) and \(\sigma \approx 0.67\). A 1,000-word document costs about 1,500 tokens on average. And because variances of independent quantities add, the total's variance is \(1000 \times 0.45 = 450\), a standard deviation of only about 21 tokens. Long documents are very predictable in token count even when single words are not.

MLE angle · why attention divides by \(\sqrt{d}\)

Two rules do a lot of work: \(\operatorname{Var}[aX] = a^2\operatorname{Var}[X]\), and variances of independent terms add. Take a query and a key with \(d\) independent entries, each with mean 0 and variance 1. Their dot product \(\sum_i q_i k_i\) is a sum of \(d\) terms of variance 1, so it has variance \(d\). With \(d = 128\) the scores have \(\sigma \approx 11\), which pushes softmax into near one-hot territory. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d}\) brings the variance back to 1. The same argument gives the \(1/\sqrt{n_{\text{in}}}\) scale used in weight initialization (Modules 04, 06).

Conditional probability and the chain rule

A language model never outputs "the probability of a sentence" directly. It outputs the probability of the next token given everything before it. That word "given" is conditional probability.

Definition · Conditional probability

\(P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A, B)}{P(B)}\), read "the probability of \(A\) given \(B\)". Restrict attention to the worlds where \(B\) happened, and ask what fraction of those also have \(A\). \(P(A, B)\) is the joint probability that both happen.

Rearranging gives \(P(A, B) = P(A)\,P(B \mid A)\). Apply that rule repeatedly to a sequence of tokens and you get the chain rule of probability. It isn't an approximation or a modelling choice: it holds for any joint distribution.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{P(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_T)} = \prod_{t=1}^{\htmlClass{t2}{T}} \htmlClass{t3}{P}\big(\htmlClass{t4}{x_t} \mid \htmlClass{t5}{x_{\lt t}}\big)$$
\(P(x_1,\ldots,x_T)\)
probability of the whole sequence
\(T\)
sequence length in tokens
\(P(\cdot\mid\cdot)\)
the next-token distribution. This is what the network computes
\(x_t\)
the token at position \(t\)
\(x_{\lt t}\)
all earlier tokens \(x_1,\ldots,x_{t-1}\): the context. For \(t=1\) it's just the start-of-sequence marker

In wordsThe probability of a sentence is the probability of its first token, times the probability of the second given the first, times the third given the first two, and so on to the end.

Why it mattersThis is the whole contract of a GPT-style model. Learn one function, "next-token distribution given a prefix", and you have a full probability model over all text. Generation is just sampling from that function one step at a time (Module 08).

Bayes' rule

Since \(P(A,B)\) can be split either way, \(P(A)P(B\mid A) = P(B)P(A\mid B)\). Divide through and you get a way to flip a conditional around.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{P(H \mid E)} = \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{P(E \mid H)}\;\htmlClass{t3}{P(H)}}{\htmlClass{t4}{P(E)}}$$
\(P(H\mid E)\)
posterior: belief in hypothesis \(H\) after seeing evidence \(E\)
\(P(E\mid H)\)
likelihood: how probable the evidence is if \(H\) is true
\(P(H)\)
prior: belief in \(H\) before the evidence
\(P(E)\)
total probability of the evidence, over all hypotheses

In wordsHow much you believe something afterwards depends on how well it explains the evidence and how plausible it was to begin with.

Why it mattersA classifier that flags "AI-generated text" catches 90% of AI text and wrongly flags 5% of human text. If 10% of submissions are AI-written, then \(P(\text{flag}) = 0.9 \times 0.1 + 0.05 \times 0.9 = 0.135\) and \(P(\text{AI} \mid \text{flag}) = 0.09 / 0.135 \approx 0.67\). A third of flagged authors are innocent. Base rates decide how far you can trust any eval judge or detector (Module 13).

Softmax: from scores to probabilities

The last layer of a transformer produces a vector of \(V\) real numbers called logits, one per vocabulary entry. They can be any size and any sign. We need non-negative numbers that sum to 1. The softmax does that.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{p_i} = \frac{\exp(\htmlClass{t2}{z_i} / \htmlClass{t3}{T})}{\sum_{j=1}^{\htmlClass{t4}{V}} \exp(\htmlClass{t2}{z_j} / \htmlClass{t3}{T})}$$
\(p_i\)
probability of token \(i\)
\(z_i\)
logit: the model's raw score for token \(i\)
\(T\)
temperature. 1 during training; a sampling knob at inference
\(V\)
vocabulary size: the sum runs over every token

In wordsExponentiate every score so it's positive, then divide by the total so they sum to 1. Bigger score, bigger share, and the exponential makes the gaps dramatic: a logit 1 higher means \(e \approx 2.72\times\) more probability.

Why it mattersOnly differences between logits matter: \(p_i / p_j = e^{(z_i - z_j)/T}\). Add the same constant to every logit and the probabilities don't move, because \(e^{c/T}\) appears in the numerator and the denominator and cancels. That's what makes the stable implementation below legal.

Think first: what happens if you multiply every logit by 2?
Multiplying by 2 is the same as dividing by \(T = 0.5\). Every gap between logits doubles, so the distribution gets sharper: the top token gains share and the tail shrinks. So softmax is invariant to shifting logits but not to scaling them. Temperature is a scaling knob. Try it in the widget: set T to 0.5 and compare with the logits doubled at T = 1.
Pitfall · softmax saturates

When one logit is far above the rest, its probability is essentially 1 and the others are essentially 0, and the gradients through the softmax become tiny. That's the failure the \(\sqrt{d}\) scaling in attention prevents. It's also why training code passes logits to the loss rather than probabilities: the loss can then be computed in log space without ever forming the saturated values.

Log-probabilities: why everything is in logs

A 1,000-token document where the model gives each token a probability of about 0.13 has probability \(0.13^{1000} \approx 10^{-886}\). The smallest positive float32 is about \(1.4 \times 10^{-45}\), and the smallest float64 is about \(4.9 \times 10^{-324}\). Multiply the probabilities one by one and you get exactly 0 long before the end. That's underflow.

Take logs and the product becomes a sum: \(\log P(x_{1:T}) = \sum_t \log P(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\). Each term is a modest negative number, around \(-2\), and the sum is about \(-2040\), which any float can hold. Logs help in three other ways too. Sums are easier to differentiate than products. A sum over tokens splits cleanly across a minibatch. And \(-\log p\) has a meaning of its own, the surprise of an outcome in nats, which the rest of this module builds on.

The log-sum-exp trick

Softmax has the opposite problem: overflow. \(e^{z}\) exceeds the largest float16 (65,504) once \(z \gt 11.09\), float32 once \(z \gt 88.7\), float64 once \(z \gt 709.8\). Logits of 20 or 30 are routine. The fix uses the shift invariance from the previous section.

$$\log \sum_{j} e^{\htmlClass{t1}{z_j}} = \htmlClass{t2}{m} + \log \sum_{j} e^{\htmlClass{t1}{z_j} - \htmlClass{t2}{m}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t2}{m} = \max_j \htmlClass{t1}{z_j}, \qquad \htmlClass{t3}{\log p_i} = \htmlClass{t1}{z_i} - \operatorname{LSE}(\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{z}})$$
\(z_j\)
logits
\(m\)
the largest logit. Subtracting it makes every exponent \(\le 0\), so every \(e^{(\cdot)}\) lies in \((0, 1]\)
\(\log p_i\)
log-softmax: the log-probability of token \(i\), computed without ever forming \(p_i\)

In wordsPull the biggest logit out front before exponentiating. Nothing overflows, because the largest exponent is \(e^0 = 1\), and at least one term is exactly 1, so the sum can't underflow to 0 either.

Why it matterstorch.logsumexp, F.log_softmax and F.cross_entropy all do this internally. Writing torch.log(torch.softmax(z)) yourself skips it and gives -inf as soon as any probability rounds to 0.

Maximum likelihood: what training optimizes

A model with parameters \(\theta\) assigns a probability \(p_\theta(x)\) to every possible text \(x\). Training has to choose \(\theta\). The standard answer, used by essentially every pretraining run: pick the parameters under which the training data was most probable.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\theta^\star} = \arg\max_{\htmlClass{t2}{\theta}} \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \log p_{\htmlClass{t2}{\theta}}\big(\htmlClass{t4}{x^{(i)}}\big) = \arg\min_{\htmlClass{t2}{\theta}} \; -\frac{1}{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \log p_{\htmlClass{t2}{\theta}}\big(\htmlClass{t4}{x^{(i)}}\big)$$
\(\theta^\star\)
the maximum-likelihood parameters
\(\theta\)
all model parameters (billions of them for an LLM)
\(N\)
number of training examples. For an LM, count every token position
\(x^{(i)}\)
one training example. For an LM, a token given its prefix: \(\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})\)

In wordsMake the observed data as unsurprising as possible. Maximizing the total log-probability is the same as minimizing the average negative log-probability, and that average is the number your training loop prints as "loss".

Why it mattersLogs turn a product over trillions of tokens into a sum, and a sum's gradient is the sum of per-token gradients. That's what lets you estimate it from a random minibatch and split it across GPUs.

Cross-entropy: the loss function

For a single position, the data says "the next token was \(y\)". The model says "here's my distribution \(q\)". The loss compares the two.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{H(p, q)} = -\sum_{x} \htmlClass{t2}{p(x)}\,\log \htmlClass{t3}{q(x)} \quad\xrightarrow{\;p\ =\ \text{one-hot at } y\;}\quad \htmlClass{t4}{\mathcal{L}} = -\log \htmlClass{t3}{q(y)}$$
\(H(p,q)\)
cross-entropy of model \(q\) measured against true distribution \(p\)
\(p(x)\)
the target distribution. For next-token training it's one-hot: 1 on the actual token, 0 elsewhere
\(q(x)\)
the model's softmax output
\(\mathcal{L}\)
per-token loss: negative log-probability (NLL) of the correct token

In wordsWith a one-hot target, every term but one is multiplied by zero. The loss is just "minus the log of the probability you gave the right answer". \(q(y) = 1\) costs 0. \(q(y) = 0.5\) costs 0.69 nats. \(q(y) = 0.01\) costs 4.6. \(q(y) \to 0\) costs \(\infty\).

Why it mattersAveraged over the dataset this is exactly the maximum-likelihood objective above. "Cross-entropy loss", "NLL", and "negative log-likelihood" are the same number in LM training.

The gradient of that loss with respect to the logits is unusually clean. Write \(\mathcal{L} = -z_y + \log\sum_j e^{z_j}\). The derivative of the second term with respect to \(z_i\) is \(e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j} = q_i\), and the first contributes \(-1\) only at \(i = y\).

$$\frac{\partial \htmlClass{t1}{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial \htmlClass{t2}{z_i}} = \htmlClass{t3}{q_i} - \htmlClass{t4}{y_i}$$
\(\mathcal{L}\)
cross-entropy loss for one position
\(z_i\)
logit for token \(i\)
\(q_i\)
model's probability for token \(i\) (softmax output)
\(y_i\)
one-hot target: 1 for the true token, 0 otherwise

In wordsPredicted minus actual. Gradient descent subtracts this, so it raises the correct token's logit by \(1 - q_y\) and lowers every wrong token's logit in proportion to how much probability it stole. Once the model is confident and right, the gradient goes to zero by itself.

Why it mattersThis is the starting signal of backpropagation in every LLM (Module 04). The components always sum to zero, because the \(q_i\) sum to 1 and so do the \(y_i\). That matches shift invariance: pushing all logits up together does nothing.

MLE angle · the first-step sanity check

A freshly initialized model has logits near zero, so its softmax is close to uniform and its loss should be close to \(-\log(1/V) = \log V\): 10.82 for GPT-2's 50,257 tokens, 11.76 for Llama 3's 128,256. If step 0 prints 25, your init scale is too big. If it prints 0.3, you're probably leaking the targets into the inputs, usually through a missing shift or a missing causal mask.

Entropy: how uncertain a distribution is

Define the surprise of an outcome as \(-\log p\). A certain event (\(p=1\)) is no surprise at all; a one-in-a-million event carries about 13.8 nats (19.9 bits). Entropy is the average surprise.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{H(p)} = -\sum_{x} \htmlClass{t2}{p(x)}\,\htmlClass{t3}{\log p(x)} \;\le\; \log \htmlClass{t4}{V}$$
\(H(p)\)
entropy: expected surprise under \(p\)
\(p(x)\)
probability of outcome \(x\). It's also the weight in the average
\(\log p(x)\)
log-probability. Natural log gives nats, \(\log_2\) gives bits. 1 nat \(= 1/\ln 2 \approx 1.443\) bits
\(V\)
number of possible outcomes. The maximum, \(\log V\), is reached only by the uniform distribution

In wordsHow many yes/no questions (bits) you'd need, on average, to pin down the outcome. A one-hot distribution has entropy 0. A fair coin has 1 bit. Uniform over 8 tokens has 3 bits. Uniform over Llama 3's vocabulary has \(\log_2 128{,}256 \approx 16.97\) bits.

Why it mattersCross-entropy is always at least entropy: \(H(p,q) \ge H(p)\). The entropy of natural text is a floor that no model's loss can go below. It's the "irreducible loss" term in scaling laws (Module 07).

Intuition

Entropy of the model's own next-token distribution is a useful runtime signal. After "The capital of France is", entropy is low because one token dominates. At the start of a creative story it's high. Some decoding methods and uncertainty detectors use exactly this number. Low entropy doesn't mean correct, though: a model can be confidently wrong.

KL divergence: distance between distributions

Cross-entropy mixes two things: the randomness that's in the data, and the extra cost of using the wrong distribution. KL divergence is just the extra cost.

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\htmlClass{t1}{p} \,\Vert\, \htmlClass{t2}{q}) = \sum_x \htmlClass{t1}{p(x)} \log \frac{\htmlClass{t1}{p(x)}}{\htmlClass{t2}{q(x)}} = \htmlClass{t3}{H(p, q)} - \htmlClass{t4}{H(p)} \;\ge\; 0$$
\(p\)
the reference distribution. The average is taken over samples from \(p\)
\(q\)
the approximating distribution
\(H(p,q)\)
cross-entropy: average surprise when outcomes come from \(p\) but you score them with \(q\)
\(H(p)\)
entropy of \(p\): the unavoidable part

In wordsThe extra nats per sample you pay for believing \(q\) when the truth is \(p\). It's zero only when \(q = p\). It is not symmetric: \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q) \ne D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p)\) in general, so it isn't a true distance.

Why it mattersSince \(H(p)\) of the data doesn't depend on the model, minimizing cross-entropy is the same as minimizing \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\text{data}}\Vert q_\theta)\). Pretraining is KL minimization in the "forward" direction.

The asymmetry matters

Look at where each direction puts its weight. Forward KL, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\), averages over samples from \(p\). Wherever \(p\) has mass and \(q\) has almost none, \(\log(p/q)\) explodes. So \(q\) is forced to cover everything \(p\) can do, even if that means putting mass in places \(p\) never goes. Reverse KL, \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p)\), averages over samples from \(q\). Now the explosion happens wherever \(q\) puts mass that \(p\) lacks. So \(q\) stays where \(p\) is solid and is free to ignore whole modes.

Forward · \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\)

Mass-covering

Used by maximum-likelihood pretraining and SFT, and by classic distillation (Hinton et al., 2015), where the student matches the teacher's softened probabilities on a fixed transfer set. A student too small to fit the teacher spreads probability over everything the teacher might say, and can end up generating text the teacher never would.

Reverse · \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\Vert p)\)

Mode-seeking

The RLHF penalty \(\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}})\) is in this direction: it's averaged over the policy's own samples and keeps the tuned model from drifting into text the reference finds absurd (Module 10). Some LLM distillation methods, such as MiniLLM (Gu et al., 2023), deliberately switch to reverse KL so a small student commits to the teacher's main modes.

Estimating KL from samples

RL libraries usually can't sum over the whole vocabulary for every token of every sampled response. Instead they estimate KL from the sampled tokens, e.g. \(\log \pi_\theta(x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(x)\) per token. That single-sample estimate is unbiased but can be negative on any one sample, so don't be alarmed when a logged "KL" dips below zero. Different libraries use different estimators; check which one yours logs before comparing runs.

Perplexity

Loss in nats is the natural unit for optimization, but hard to feel. Perplexity turns it back into a count.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\text{PPL}} = e^{\htmlClass{t2}{\mathcal{L}}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t2}{\mathcal{L}} = -\frac{1}{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \sum_{t=1}^{\htmlClass{t3}{N}} \htmlClass{t4}{\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{\lt t})}$$
PPL
perplexity. Lower is better; 1 means perfect prediction
\(\mathcal{L}\)
mean negative log-likelihood per token, in nats (the eval loss). With bits, use \(2^{\mathcal{L}}\) instead
\(N\)
number of predicted tokens in the evaluation text
\(\log p_\theta(\cdot)\)
the model's log-probability for each actual next token

In wordsThe geometric mean of \(1/p\) over the tokens. If the model were choosing uniformly among \(k\) equally likely tokens at every step, its perplexity would be exactly \(k\). So perplexity is the effective branching factor: "as unsure as a fair \(k\)-sided die, on average".

Why it mattersThe six-token sentence in Fig 03.2 has mean NLL 2.00 nats, so its perplexity is about 7.4. Because perplexity is exponential in loss, a fixed loss drop of 0.05 nats always cuts perplexity by the same ~4.9%, whether you start at 20 or at 5.

Only compare perplexities that share a tokenizer

Perplexity is per token, and tokens differ between tokenizers. A tokenizer with bigger tokens covers the same text in fewer, harder predictions: higher loss per token, possibly lower loss per document. Two models with different vocabularies, or the same model on text in a language its tokenizer splits finely, can't be ranked by perplexity. Normalize by text length instead: bits per byte \(= \frac{\sum_t -\log_2 p(x_t\mid x_{\lt t})}{\text{number of UTF-8 bytes}}\) is tokenizer-independent. Chat templates, the eval text, and the context length all change perplexity too, so hold them fixed.

Sampling primer: from distribution to text

A trained model gives you a distribution at each step. To actually write text, you have to pick a token, append it, and run again. Three basic choices:

  • Greedy takes \(\arg\max_i p_i\) every time. It's deterministic and fine for short extraction answers, but on long outputs it tends to loop and repeat itself.
  • Sampling draws the token at random with probability \(p_i\). Over many generations you reproduce the model's distribution, including its long tail of odd choices.
  • Temperature rescales logits before the softmax. \(T \lt 1\) sharpens toward greedy (\(T \to 0\) is greedy), \(T \gt 1\) flattens toward uniform. It changes the distribution's entropy but never the ranking of tokens.

Production decoders add truncation on top: top-k keeps the \(k\) most likely tokens, top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set whose probability reaches \(p\), min-p drops tokens below a fraction of the top probability. Then they renormalize and sample. All of these, plus repetition penalties and speculative decoding, are in Module 08 · Sampling.

Pitfall · "temperature 0" isn't always reproducible

Greedy decoding is deterministic in math, but serving stacks batch your request with others, and floating-point sums in different orders can round differently. When two tokens are nearly tied, that's enough to flip the argmax and send the rest of the generation down a different path. Don't build tests that assume byte-identical outputs from an API at \(T = 0\).

Hands-on

1. Log-softmax and cross-entropy on [B, T, V] logits

A causal LM returns logits of shape [batch, seq, vocab]. The logits at position \(t\) predict the token at \(t+1\), so you shift before computing the loss. F.cross_entropy wants class scores on dimension 1, so the usual move is to flatten batch and sequence into one axis.


import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F

torch.manual_seed(0)
B, T, V = 2, 6, 50
logits = torch.randn(B, T, V)              # model output: one score per vocab entry
tokens = torch.randint(0, V, (B, T))       # the input token ids

# position t predicts token t+1: drop the last logit, drop the first token
pred = logits[:, :-1, :]                   # [B, T-1, V]
target = tokens[:, 1:]                     # [B, T-1]

# (a) by hand with log_softmax (uses log-sum-exp internally)
logp = F.log_softmax(pred, dim=-1)                         # [B, T-1, V]
nll = -logp.gather(-1, target.unsqueeze(-1)).squeeze(-1)   # [B, T-1], one loss per token
loss_manual = nll.mean()

# (b) the library call: flatten to [N, V] logits and [N] targets
loss = F.cross_entropy(pred.reshape(-1, V), target.reshape(-1))
assert torch.allclose(loss, loss_manual, atol=1e-6)

print(f"loss {loss.item():.4f} nats   uniform would be ln V = {torch.log(torch.tensor(float(V))).item():.4f}")
print(f"perplexity {torch.exp(loss).item():.2f}")

# padding: label it -100 and cross_entropy skips it (ignore_index=-100 by default)
target_padded = target.clone()
target_padded[1, -2:] = -100
loss_masked = F.cross_entropy(pred.reshape(-1, V), target_padded.reshape(-1))
print(f"masked loss {loss_masked.item():.4f} (mean over the {(target_padded != -100).sum().item()} real tokens)")

# the gradient really is softmax minus one-hot
z = torch.tensor([[2.0, 1.0, 0.1]], requires_grad=True)
F.cross_entropy(z, torch.tensor([0])).backward()
print(z.grad)                                       # tensor([[-0.3410,  0.2424,  0.0986]])
print(torch.softmax(z, -1) - torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]))

3. Perplexity of a Hugging Face causal LM on your own text

Passing labels=input_ids makes the model shift internally and return the mean NLL over the \(T-1\) predicted tokens. The second half recomputes it from the logits so you can see nothing magic is happening.


import math
import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

name = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B"                  # any causal LM works
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16, device_map="auto")  # newer releases also accept dtype=
model.eval()

text = open("sample.txt", encoding="utf-8").read()
ids = tok(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids[:, :1024].to(model.device)  # stay within context

with torch.no_grad():
    out = model(ids, labels=ids)            # HF shifts labels by one internally

nll = out.loss.item()                       # mean nats per predicted token
print(f"tokens {ids.shape[1]}  loss {nll:.3f} nats  ({nll / math.log(2):.3f} bits)  ppl {math.exp(nll):.2f}")

# same number from the logits by hand
logits = out.logits[:, :-1].float()         # position t predicts token t+1
target = ids[:, 1:]
nll_manual = F.cross_entropy(logits.reshape(-1, logits.size(-1)), target.reshape(-1))
print(f"manual loss {nll_manual.item():.3f}")

# tokenizer-independent: bits per UTF-8 byte of the predicted text (approximate)
total_bits = nll_manual.item() * target.numel() / math.log(2)
n_bytes = len(tok.decode(target[0]).encode("utf-8"))
print(f"bits per byte {total_bits / n_bytes:.3f}")

A model outputs logits \((1,\ 1,\ 1 + \ln 2)\) over three tokens, at temperature 1. What are the probabilities?

  1. (0.33, 0.33, 0.33)
  2. (0.30, 0.30, 0.40)
  3. (0.25, 0.25, 0.50)
  4. (0.21, 0.21, 0.58)
Subtract 1 from every logit (allowed, softmax is shift-invariant): \((0, 0, \ln 2)\). Exponentiate: \((1, 1, 2)\). Divide by the sum, 4: \((0.25, 0.25, 0.5)\).

The model gives the correct next token a probability of 0.25. What is the cross-entropy loss on that token?

  1. 0.25 nats
  2. 1.39 nats, which is exactly 2 bits
  3. 0.75 nats
  4. 4 nats
\(-\ln 0.25 = \ln 4 \approx 1.386\) nats, and \(-\log_2 0.25 = 2\) bits. It's the same surprise as guessing among 4 equally likely options. 0.75 is \(1 - p\), which is the size of the gradient on the target logit, not the loss.

Your eval loss is 2.3 nats per token. Roughly what is the perplexity?

  1. 2.3
  2. 4.6
  3. 0.1
  4. About 10
\(e^{2.3} \approx 9.97\). On average the model is as unsure as a uniform choice among about 10 tokens. 0.1 is \(e^{-2.3}\), the geometric-mean probability of the correct token.

You initialize a model with the GPT-2 vocabulary (50,257 tokens) and near-zero logits. What should the loss be on the very first step?

  1. About 10.8
  2. About 0.69
  3. About 1.0
  4. About 50,257
Near-zero logits give a near-uniform softmax, so the loss is \(-\ln(1/V) = \ln 50{,}257 \approx 10.82\), the entropy of the uniform distribution. 50,257 would be the perplexity, not the loss. A very different number at step 0 usually means a bug.

You fit a single Gaussian \(q\) to a target \(p\) that has two well-separated humps, by minimizing \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q \Vert p)\) (reverse KL). What do you get?

  1. A wide Gaussian covering both humps
  2. Exactly \(p\)
  3. A narrow Gaussian sitting on one hump
  4. A narrow Gaussian in the valley between the humps
Reverse KL averages over samples from \(q\) and is huge wherever \(q\) puts mass that \(p\) lacks, so \(q\) avoids the valley and commits to one mode. A single Gaussian can't equal a two-hump mixture. The wide, mass-covering fit is what forward KL \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\Vert q)\) gives you.

After softmax the model's probabilities are \((0.7, 0.2, 0.1)\), and the true token is the second one. What is \(\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\mathbf{z}\)?

  1. (0.7, 0.8, 0.1)
  2. (−0.7, 0.8, −0.1)
  3. (0, −0.8, 0)
  4. (0.7, −0.8, 0.1)
\(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{y} = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1) - (0, 1, 0) = (0.7, -0.8, 0.1)\). The components sum to 0. A descent step raises the true token's logit and lowers the others, mostly the first one, which took the most probability.

On the same English test file, model A (32K-token vocabulary) has perplexity 9 and model B (128K-token vocabulary) has perplexity 12. Which model predicts the text better?

  1. A, because lower perplexity always wins
  2. You can't tell from these numbers. Compare total NLL per byte or per character instead
  3. B, because a larger vocabulary is always better
  4. They're equivalent, since 9 and 12 are close
B's tokens are probably longer, so it makes fewer predictions over the same text, each one harder. Per-token perplexity penalizes that. Total NLL divided by the number of bytes (bits per byte) puts both on the same scale.
