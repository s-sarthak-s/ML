Module 15 · Beyond text

Generating images, audio & video: pictures out of noise

Type a sentence, wait a few seconds, and get a photo that never existed. Nearly every current image and video model gets there the same odd way: it starts from pure static and removes noise over 1 to 50 passes of a neural network, steered by your text. This module builds that idea from nothing, gives you a real working sampler to break, and then scales it up to latent space, transformers, video, and audio.

  6. Evaluate generated media with FID, CLIP score, and human preference, and name each one's blind spot.

Three ways to generate

A 1024×1024 colour image is 3,145,728 numbers. A generative model has to learn which settings of those numbers look like photographs, then produce new ones on demand. Language models handle the text version by predicting one token at a time (Module 06), and the most direct option for images copies them: chop the picture into discrete tokens and predict those one after another. That is autoregressive generation.

The family that won images and video works differently. A diffusion (or flow) model learns to remove a little noise from a corrupted image; to sample, you start from pure noise and apply the denoiser over and over. The third family, GANs (Goodfellow et al., 2014), pairs a generator that maps a noise vector to an image in a single pass with a discriminator trained to catch its fakes. The two networks compete until the fakes pass.

GANs led image generation from about 2016 to 2020. Diffusion models overtook them on sample quality (Dhariwal & Nichol, 2021, titled their paper "Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis"), mainly because a regression loss is far easier to train at scale than a two-player game. The adversarial loss did not disappear, though. It now lives inside other systems: it sharpens VAE decoders, and it powers the distillation tricks that let modern models sample in 1 to 4 steps (section 10).

Intuition

Diffusion is a sculptor that starts from a block of static instead of marble. Each pass the network asks "what noise is sitting on top of a real image here?" and chips a little of it away. Early passes decide the composition; late passes add texture. You will see that order emerge in the widgets below.

Autoencoders, VAEs, and latent space

Pixels are wasteful. Neighbouring pixels are almost always similar, and most of the 3 million numbers in a photo are predictable from the others. So before generating anything, current systems compress images with an autoencoder: an encoder \(E\) squeezes the image into a small grid of numbers called the latent, and a decoder \(D\) reconstructs the image from it. The generative model then works entirely in that latent space.

A plain autoencoder is trained only to reconstruct, so its latent space can be full of holes: points that decode to garbage. A variational autoencoder (VAE; Kingma & Welling, 2013) fixes that by making the encoder output a distribution \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) (a mean and a standard deviation per latent number) and by penalising it for straying from a simple prior \(p(z)=\mathcal{N}(0,I)\).

$$\log \htmlClass{t1}{p_\theta(x)} \;\ge\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{z\sim \htmlClass{t2}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}}\big[\log \htmlClass{t3}{p_\theta(x\mid z)}\big]}_{\text{reconstruction}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(\htmlClass{t2}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\,\big\|\,\htmlClass{t4}{p(z)}\big)}_{\text{stay close to the prior}}$$
\(p_\theta(x)\)
probability the model assigns to a real image \(x\). We want it high, but it's intractable to compute directly
\(q_\phi(z\mid x)\)
the encoder: a Gaussian over latents for this image, with mean \(\mu_\phi(x)\) and std \(\sigma_\phi(x)\)
\(p_\theta(x\mid z)\)
the decoder: how well the latent reconstructs the image. With a Gaussian decoder this is a squared-error term
\(p(z)\)
the prior, a standard normal \(\mathcal{N}(0, I)\)

In wordsMaximise reconstruction quality, minus a penalty for encoding images into weird, spread-out corners of latent space. The sum is a lower bound on the log-likelihood, so pushing it up pushes the likelihood up.

Why it mattersThe KL term organises the latent space so nearby latents decode to similar images. That smoothness is what lets a diffusion model wander through latent space and land on something decodable.

Training needs gradients through the sampling step \(z\sim q_\phi(z\mid x)\), and you can't differentiate through a random draw. The fix is to move the randomness outside the network:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{z} = \htmlClass{t2}{\mu_\phi(x)} + \htmlClass{t3}{\sigma_\phi(x)} \odot \htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$
\(z\)
the sampled latent passed to the decoder
\(\mu_\phi(x)\)
encoder's predicted mean (a deterministic function of the image)
\(\sigma_\phi(x)\)
encoder's predicted standard deviation; \(\odot\) is element-wise multiplication
\(\varepsilon\)
parameter-free standard normal noise, drawn fresh each time

In wordsInstead of "sample from a distribution the network chose", write "take fixed random noise, scale it, shift it". Now \(z\) is a differentiable function of \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\), and backprop works.

Why it mattersThe same pattern, a clean signal plus scaled Gaussian noise, is the entire forward process of diffusion in the next section.

The arithmetic is why latent diffusion was a breakthrough (Rombach et al., 2022). Stable Diffusion 1.x uses a downsampling factor of 8 and 4 latent channels:

\(512\times512\times3 = 786{,}432\) numbers \(\;\longrightarrow\;\) \(64\times64\times4 = 16{,}384\) numbers, which is 48× fewer.

Every denoising pass now runs on 48× fewer numbers. Newer models (SD3, FLUX.1) keep the 8× spatial downsampling but use 16 channels. That is a gentler 12× compression, and it buys back fine detail such as small text and faces.

Not the textbook VAE

The autoencoders inside image generators are trained mostly for reconstruction. They use a very small KL weight, plus a perceptual loss (LPIPS) and a patch-based adversarial loss, a recipe inherited from VQGAN (Esser et al., 2021). The KL term only keeps the latent well scaled. On its own, sampling \(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and decoding would give mush. The diffusion model does the generating, and the VAE is a learned compression codec.

Pitfall · the latent scaling factor

VAE latents aren't unit-variance, so pipelines multiply them by a constant before diffusion and divide it back out before decoding (Stable Diffusion 1.x: 0.18215, stored as vae.config.scaling_factor). If you hand-decode latents and forget it, you get saturated garbage. Newer VAEs also use a shift_factor.

The forward process: destroying an image on purpose

Here is the core trick of diffusion (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015; made practical by Ho, Jain & Abbeel, 2020, "DDPM"). Destroying information is easy: add a bit of Gaussian noise, then a bit more, for \(T=1000\) steps, until nothing but static remains. If we can train a network to reverse one small step of that destruction, we can chain the reversals and walk from static back to an image.

Each forward step mixes in noise with variance \(\beta_t\) and shrinks the signal slightly so the total variance stays at 1. Composing many Gaussian steps gives another Gaussian, so you can jump straight from the clean image to any step \(t\) in one shot:

$$q(\htmlClass{t1}{x_t}\mid \htmlClass{t2}{x_0}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\htmlClass{t3}{\bar\alpha_t}}\,\htmlClass{t2}{x_0},\;(1-\htmlClass{t3}{\bar\alpha_t})\,I\big) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \htmlClass{t1}{x_t} = \sqrt{\htmlClass{t3}{\bar\alpha_t}}\,\htmlClass{t2}{x_0} + \sqrt{1-\htmlClass{t3}{\bar\alpha_t}}\;\htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon}$$
\(x_t\)
the noisy image at step \(t\) (or a noisy latent, in latent diffusion)
\(x_0\)
the clean training image, scaled to roughly unit variance
\(\bar\alpha_t\)
"alpha-bar": the fraction of signal variance left at step \(t\). 1 at \(t=0\), close to 0 at \(t=T\)
\(\varepsilon\)
standard Gaussian noise, same shape as the image

In wordsA noisy image is a weighted blend of the clean image and pure noise. The weights are chosen so their squares sum to 1, which is why this is called a variance-preserving process.

Why it mattersTraining never simulates the 1000-step chain. It picks a random \(t\), makes \(x_t\) in one line, and moves on. At \(\bar\alpha=0.64\): \(x_t = 0.8\,x_0 + 0.6\,\varepsilon\).

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\bar\alpha_t} = \prod_{s=1}^{t}\big(1-\htmlClass{t2}{\beta_s}\big), \qquad \htmlClass{t3}{\mathrm{SNR}(t)} = \frac{\htmlClass{t1}{\bar\alpha_t}}{1-\htmlClass{t1}{\bar\alpha_t}}$$
\(\bar\alpha_t\)
cumulative signal fraction after \(t\) steps
\(\beta_s\)
noise variance added at step \(s\). The schedule is the choice of these numbers
\(\mathrm{SNR}\)
signal power over noise power. 1 (0 dB) means equal parts image and static

In wordsEach step keeps a fraction \(1-\beta\) of the remaining signal; multiply those fractions together to get what's left. The schedule decides how fast the image dissolves.

Why it mattersDDPM's linear schedule (\(\beta\) from \(10^{-4}\) to 0.02) dissolves images fast: \(\bar\alpha\) hits 0.5 around \(t\approx260\), and by \(t=500\) it's 0.08. Much of the last half of the chain is nearly pure noise, which wastes training and sampling effort. The cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021) reaches \(\bar\alpha=0.5\) near \(t\approx500\) instead.

Think first: at SNR = 1 (0 dB), can you still see the picture?
Yes, roughly. Per pixel, signal and noise have equal power. But the noise is independent from pixel to pixel while the image is smooth, so your eye averages over a patch of pixels and the noise partly cancels. Coarse shapes survive long after fine texture is gone. This is also why higher resolutions need noise schedules shifted toward more noise: with more pixels per object, the same per-pixel SNR leaves more of the composition visible, so the model never practises inventing it from scratch (Hoogeboom et al., 2023; SD3 uses a resolution-dependent shift, Esser et al., 2024).

Learning to denoise

The training recipe fits in five lines. Take a real image \(x_0\). Pick a random step \(t\) and a random noise sample \(\varepsilon\). Build \(x_t\) with the forward formula. Show the network \(x_t\) and \(t\), and ask it to guess which noise was added. Score the guess with squared error.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathcal{L}} = \mathbb{E}_{\htmlClass{t2}{x_0},\,\htmlClass{t3}{t},\,\htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon}}\Big[\big\lVert \htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon} - \htmlClass{t5}{\varepsilon_\theta}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\htmlClass{t2}{x_0} + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon},\; \htmlClass{t3}{t}\big)\big\rVert^2\Big]$$
\(\mathcal{L}\)
training loss: a plain mean-squared error
\(x_0\)
a training image (or latent)
\(t\)
a random step, uniform over 1…T; the network is told \(t\) so it knows how noisy its input is
\(\varepsilon\)
the noise that was actually added: the regression target
\(\varepsilon_\theta\)
the denoiser network (U-Net or transformer), which outputs a tensor the same shape as the image

In wordsCorrupt an image by a random amount, then train the network to name the corruption. No adversary, no sampling during training, just regression.

Why it mattersThis stability is the reason diffusion scaled where GANs struggled. The loss curve goes down smoothly, and more data and compute reliably help.

Why predicting the noise is enough

Given \(x_t\) and a noise estimate \(\hat\varepsilon\), you can solve the forward equation for the clean image: \(\hat x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\). Predicting noise and predicting the image are the same task in different units. The network trained with squared error learns the average answer, \(\mathbb{E}[x_0\mid x_t]\). At high noise that average is a blur of every image consistent with the static; at low noise it's sharp. Sampling works by asking for that average, taking a small step toward it, and asking again with less noise. Each time, the set of consistent images narrows.

There's a second reading. The noise prediction is, up to a scale, the score: the direction in which the data density increases fastest (Vincent, 2011; Song & Ermon, 2019; Song et al., 2021, unified the two views).

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\nabla_{x_t}\log p_t(x_t)} \;\approx\; -\,\frac{\htmlClass{t2}{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}}{\htmlClass{t3}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}}$$
\(\nabla \log p_t\)
the score of the noised data distribution: an arrow at every point, pointing toward more likely images
\(\varepsilon_\theta\)
the network's noise estimate
\(\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\)
the noise standard deviation at step \(t\)

In words"Which noise was added?" and "which way is uphill toward real data?" have the same answer with opposite signs. Subtract the predicted noise and you climb toward the data.

Why it mattersFor a single training pair it's exact: \(\nabla\log q(x_t\mid x_0) = -\varepsilon/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\). Averaged over all images, the trained network approximates the true score. That turns sampling into solving a differential equation, which is why "samplers" and "solvers" are interchangeable words in diffusion libraries.

Sampling: DDPM and DDIM

The original DDPM sampler walks back through all 1000 steps. Each step removes predicted noise and then adds a little fresh noise, so sampling is stochastic. DDIM (Song, Meng & Ermon, 2020) showed the same trained network supports a deterministic update that can skip steps: estimate the clean image, then re-noise it to a less noisy level using the predicted noise instead of new random noise.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\hat x_0} = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\htmlClass{t2}{\hat\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}, \qquad \htmlClass{t3}{x_{t'}} = \sqrt{\htmlClass{t4}{\bar\alpha_{t'}}}\;\htmlClass{t1}{\hat x_0} + \sqrt{1-\htmlClass{t4}{\bar\alpha_{t'}}}\;\htmlClass{t2}{\hat\varepsilon}$$
\(\hat x_0\)
current best guess of the clean image (blurry early, sharp late)
\(\hat\varepsilon\)
predicted noise \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\), reused for the jump
\(x_{t'}\)
the next, less noisy state; \(t' \lt t\), and the gap can be large
\(\bar\alpha_{t'}\)
signal fraction at the target step

In wordsGuess the finished picture, then rebuild a slightly-less-noisy version of it using the same noise you think is there. Repeat with a smaller noise level.

Why it mattersThis is how 1000 training steps became 20–50 sampling steps without retraining. In diffusers, the scheduler object implements exactly this kind of update.

Definition · prediction targets

Networks can be trained to output the noise \(\varepsilon\) (DDPM), the clean image \(x_0\), or the "velocity" \(v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0\) (Salimans & Ho, 2022). All three carry the same information given \(x_t\). They differ in how well-conditioned the regression is at the extremes. Predicting \(\varepsilon\) at nearly zero noise is easy but useless; predicting it at pure noise is trivial. The next section's flow matching uses a velocity target.

A working diffusion sampler in 2D

Real denoisers have billions of parameters. For intuition you don't need them. Take a toy "dataset" of 2D points drawn from a mixture of Gaussian blobs. Noising a Gaussian blob gives another Gaussian blob, so the noised data at every step is still a mixture. Its mean shrinks to \(\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\mu_k\) and its variance grows to \(\bar\alpha s_k^2 + 1-\bar\alpha\). That means the perfect denoiser, the thing a network is trying to learn, can be written down exactly:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbb{E}[x_0\mid x_t]} = \sum_k \htmlClass{t2}{r_k(x_t)}\Big[\htmlClass{t3}{\mu_k} + \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\htmlClass{t4}{s_k^2}}{\bar\alpha\,\htmlClass{t4}{s_k^2} + 1-\bar\alpha}\big(x_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\htmlClass{t3}{\mu_k}\big)\Big], \qquad \htmlClass{t2}{r_k} \propto \htmlClass{t5}{\pi_k}\,\mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\htmlClass{t3}{\mu_k},\ (\bar\alpha\,\htmlClass{t4}{s_k^2} + 1-\bar\alpha)\,I\big)$$
\(\mathbb{E}[x_0\mid x_t]\)
the best possible guess of the clean point, which is what an MSE-trained denoiser converges to
\(r_k(x_t)\)
responsibility: how likely it is that this noisy point came from blob \(k\) (a softmax over blobs)
\(\mu_k\)
centre of blob \(k\) in the clean data
\(s_k^2\)
variance of blob \(k\) (isotropic here)
\(\pi_k\)
mixture weight: how much data each blob holds

In wordsWork out which blob the noisy point probably came from, then, within each blob, pull the point toward the blob's centre by an amount that depends on how noisy it is. Average those guesses by probability.

Why it mattersWith an exact denoiser, every flaw you see in the widget comes from the sampler (step count, deterministic vs stochastic), not from an imperfect model. At pure noise (\(\bar\alpha=0\)) the formula returns the overall data mean, the "average image".

What to notice. With 1 step, every particle lands on the same point: the mean of the whole dataset, in the empty middle of the ring. This is the 2D version of the grey, blurry average image you get from a one-step sampler that wasn't trained for it. With 2 to 4 steps, particles land between blobs or smeared along the ring. Each jump commits to a guess made when the picture was still ambiguous. By 10 to 20 steps the samples match the data, and more steps change very little. The stochastic DDPM sampler is worse at small step counts but keeps improving at large ones, because its fresh noise lets it correct earlier mistakes.

Think first: the trajectories never cross each other in DDIM mode. Why?
Deterministic DDIM approximates an ordinary differential equation, the probability-flow ODE (Song et al., 2021). Through any point at a given time, there's exactly one velocity, so two solution curves can't pass through the same point at the same time. That's what makes the noise-to-image map deterministic and invertible. You can run it backwards to find the noise that produces a given image, the basis of many image-editing techniques. DDPM paths do cross, because fresh noise kicks particles around.

Flow matching and rectified flow

Diffusion's noise schedule, alpha-bars, and three possible prediction targets are a lot of machinery. Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022) and rectified flow (Liu, Gong & Liu, 2022), with the closely related stochastic interpolants of Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden (2022), strip it down. Connect each data point to a noise sample with a straight line, and train a network to predict the velocity along that line.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{x_t} = (1-\htmlClass{t2}{t})\,\htmlClass{t3}{x_0} + \htmlClass{t2}{t}\,\htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon}, \qquad \htmlClass{t5}{v} = \frac{d\htmlClass{t1}{x_t}}{d\htmlClass{t2}{t}} = \htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon} - \htmlClass{t3}{x_0}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{FM}} = \mathbb{E}\,\big\lVert \htmlClass{t6}{v_\theta}(\htmlClass{t1}{x_t}, \htmlClass{t2}{t}) - (\htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon} - \htmlClass{t3}{x_0})\big\rVert^2$$
\(x_t\)
a point on the straight line between data and noise
\(t\)
time in [0, 1]: 0 is clean data, 1 is pure noise (this module's convention, also SD3's)
\(x_0\)
a training image or latent
\(\varepsilon\)
a Gaussian noise sample paired with it
\(v\)
the velocity along the line: constant, pointing from data toward noise
\(v_\theta\)
the network, trained to predict that velocity from \(x_t\) and \(t\) alone

In wordsSlide a point along a straight line from image to noise; train the network to say which way and how fast it's sliding. To generate, start at noise and run the slide backwards: \(x \leftarrow x - \Delta t\cdot v_\theta(x, t)\).

Why it mattersWorked example: \(x_0=(2,-1)\), \(\varepsilon=(0.5,0.5)\). The target is \(v=(-1.5,\,1.5)\) at every \(t\), and at \(t=0.25\) the point sits at \((1.625,\,-0.625)\). Stable Diffusion 3 (Esser et al., 2024) and FLUX.1 (Black Forest Labs, 2024) are both rectified-flow transformers.

Compare the per-sample paths. Diffusion's \(x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha}\,\varepsilon\) traces a quarter-circle arc from data to noise, because the two coefficients satisfy \(a^2+b^2=1\). Flow matching's coefficients satisfy \(a+b=1\), which gives a straight line. A straight path can be followed with one big Euler step; a curved one needs many small ones.

Straight in training is not straight in sampling

Panel (b) is the honest part. The per-sample training paths are straight, but the ODE you actually integrate averages over every pair whose lines pass through a point, and those averaged paths still bend wherever the data has several modes. Liu et al.'s reflow procedure straightens them, at the cost of extra training. With Gaussian noise, Euler steps on the flow ODE and DDIM steps on a diffusion model are the same update up to a change of variables. Run the widgets with an exact model and the two give nearly identical errors (try it in section 10). The practical gains reported for rectified flow come from the training setup. SD3, for instance, samples training timesteps from a logit-normal distribution that emphasises mid-range noise. They also come from reflow and distillation, not from the straight-line formula alone.

Pitfall · which end is noise?

Lipman et al. put noise at \(t=0\) and data at \(t=1\). Rectified-flow papers and SD3 put data at \(t=0\). The velocity sign flips with the convention. When you port a sampler or a loss between codebases, check this first. A flipped sign produces confident nonsense rather than an error.

Conditioning on text, and classifier-free guidance

An unconditional model produces random images from its training distribution. To make it follow a prompt, the denoiser also receives an encoding of the text, \(c\), and is trained on (image, caption) pairs. Two design choices matter: which text encoder, and how its output enters the denoiser.

Text encoders

Stable Diffusion 1.x used the frozen text tower of CLIP ViT-L/14 (Module 14), capped at 77 tokens. SDXL concatenates two CLIP text encoders. Imagen (Saharia et al., 2022) used a frozen T5-XXL language model and reported that a bigger text encoder improved image–text alignment more than a bigger denoiser did. SD3 and FLUX.1 combine CLIP with T5-XXL. CLIP contributes a pooled summary vector (SD3 also uses its per-token outputs); T5 contributes a sequence of per-token embeddings that copes better with long, compositional prompts and with rendering text inside images.

Getting text into the denoiser

U-Net models add cross-attention layers. The image tokens ask the questions (queries) and the text tokens supply the answers (keys and values). Transformer models like SD3's MMDiT go further: they concatenate image and text tokens and run joint attention, so information flows both ways. In both designs a pooled text vector is usually added to the timestep embedding as well.

$$\mathrm{CrossAttn}(\htmlClass{t1}{h}, \htmlClass{t2}{c}) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{(\htmlClass{t1}{h}\htmlClass{t3}{W_Q})(\htmlClass{t2}{c}\htmlClass{t3}{W_K})^{\top}}{\sqrt{\htmlClass{t4}{d}}}\Big)\,\htmlClass{t2}{c}\htmlClass{t3}{W_V}$$
\(h\)
image features: one row per spatial location (e.g. 4096 latent positions)
\(c\)
text encoder outputs: one row per prompt token (e.g. 77)
\(W_Q, W_K, W_V\)
learned projections, as in ordinary attention (Module 06)
\(d\)
head dimension

In wordsEvery patch of the image looks over the prompt, decides which words are relevant to it, and pulls in their meaning. The patch where the bicycle is forming attends to "bicycle" and "red"; the background attends to "wall".

Why it mattersThe attention map is a 4096×77 matrix per head, and you can read it. Editing methods such as Prompt-to-Prompt (Hertz et al., 2022) work by manipulating these maps directly.

Classifier-free guidance

Conditioning alone gives images that only loosely follow the prompt. The fix that every text-to-image system adopted is classifier-free guidance (CFG; Ho & Salimans, 2021). During training, replace the caption with an empty one about 10% of the time, so the same network learns both a conditional prediction \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c)\) and an unconditional one \(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)\). At sampling time, run both and extrapolate away from the unconditional one:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\hat\varepsilon} = \htmlClass{t2}{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)} + \htmlClass{t3}{w}\,\big(\htmlClass{t4}{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c)} - \htmlClass{t2}{\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)}\big)$$
\(\hat\varepsilon\)
the guided noise prediction actually used by the sampler
\(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)\)
unconditional prediction: "what noise is on some image?"
\(w\)
guidance scale (guidance_scale in diffusers). \(w=1\) is plain conditional sampling, \(w=0\) ignores the prompt
\(\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, c)\)
conditional prediction: "what noise is on an image of this prompt?"

In wordsFind the direction that the prompt adds, the difference between the conditional and unconditional guesses, and exaggerate it \(w\) times.

Why it mattersTranslated to scores, the guided model samples from \(p(x)\,p(c\mid x)^{w}\): the data distribution reweighted by an implicit classifier's confidence raised to the power \(w\). For \(w\gt1\) this favours images that are unmistakably the prompt and starves the ambiguous ones. Prompt adherence goes up and diversity goes down. At large \(w\) colours oversaturate.

In the widget, each class has three blobs and one blob of each class sits in the shared middle region. At \(w=1\) the sampler reproduces the class distribution exactly, including the middle blob. As \(w\) rises, that middle blob, the one an observer could mistake for the other class, empties out first. Its mass moves to the two unambiguous blobs, and at high \(w\) samples overshoot past the blob centres. The real-image equivalents are less varied compositions, the same "canonical" pose every time, and overcooked contrast.

MLE angle · what CFG costs

CFG needs two denoiser evaluations per step, usually batched together, so it roughly doubles compute per image. Typical scales: around 5–8 for SD 1.5 and SDXL; diffusers turns CFG off entirely when guidance_scale ≤ 1. Step-distilled models like FLUX.1-schnell run with guidance_scale=0 and no second pass. FLUX.1-dev was guidance-distilled: the guidance scale is fed in as an input, so one pass imitates the two-pass result. A negative prompt simply replaces the empty caption \(\varnothing\) with text you want to steer away from.

U-Net versus Diffusion Transformer

Early diffusion models used a U-Net (Ronneberger et al., 2015): convolutional ResNet blocks that downsample the image to coarse resolution and then upsample it back, with skip connections carrying fine detail across, and attention layers at the lower resolutions. The timestep is embedded and added inside every ResNet block.

The Diffusion Transformer (DiT; Peebles & Xie, 2022) throws the U shape away. It cuts the latent into \(p\times p\) patches (usually \(p=2\)), treats each patch as a token, and runs a plain transformer. That is the same move as ViT (Module 14) and the same block as an LLM (Module 06). A 1024×1024 image becomes a 128×128 latent, then 64×64 = 4,096 tokens. The timestep and conditioning enter through adaptive layer norm:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{h} \leftarrow \htmlClass{t1}{h} + \htmlClass{t2}{\alpha(c)} \odot F\big((1+\htmlClass{t3}{\gamma(c)})\odot \mathrm{LN}(\htmlClass{t1}{h}) + \htmlClass{t4}{\beta(c)}\big), \qquad (\htmlClass{t2}{\alpha},\htmlClass{t3}{\gamma},\htmlClass{t4}{\beta}) = \mathrm{MLP}(\htmlClass{t5}{c})$$
\(h\)
token hidden states (the residual stream)
\(\alpha(c)\)
per-channel gate on the block's output, initialised to zero ("-Zero")
\(\gamma(c)\)
per-channel scale applied after layer norm
\(\beta(c)\)
per-channel shift applied after layer norm
\(c\)
conditioning vector: timestep embedding plus class label or pooled text embedding

In wordsThe noise level doesn't enter as a token. It turns the knobs on every layer norm: how much to scale each channel, how much to shift it, and how much of each block's output to let through. \(F\) is the attention or MLP sub-block.

Why it mattersWith the gates starting at zero, every block begins as the identity function, which made deep DiTs train stably. The DiT paper found that sample quality improved steadily with model compute, the same kind of scaling behaviour that made transformers dominant in language.

Transformers won for engineering reasons as much as quality. One block type scales the way LLM practice already understands, with the same parallelism, kernels, and FlashAttention. Variable resolutions and aspect ratios are just different token counts. Text and image tokens can share attention. And video is the same model with more tokens, which is the subject of section 11.

Making it fast: cost and few-step models

Generation latency is dominated by one loop, so the cost model is short:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{T_{\text{gen}}} \approx \htmlClass{t2}{N_{\text{steps}}}\cdot \htmlClass{t3}{k_{\text{CFG}}}\cdot \htmlClass{t4}{T_{\text{fwd}}} \;+\; \htmlClass{t5}{T_{\text{text}} + T_{\text{VAE}}}$$
\(T_{\text{gen}}\)
wall time for one image
\(N_{\text{steps}}\)
sampler steps (num_inference_steps)
\(k_{\text{CFG}}\)
2 with classifier-free guidance, 1 without (or with guidance distillation)
\(T_{\text{fwd}}\)
time for one denoiser forward pass
\(T_{\text{text}}, T_{\text{VAE}}\)
one-off costs: encode the prompt, decode the latent

In wordsTime is steps × passes per step × time per pass, plus a little overhead. Halve the steps and you nearly halve the time.

Why it mattersExample: 50 steps with CFG at 60 ms per pass is 50 × 2 × 0.06 = 6 s. A 4-step distilled model without CFG is 4 × 1 × 0.06 = 0.24 s, 25× faster on the same hardware.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\text{FLOPs}_{\text{fwd}}} \approx 2\,\htmlClass{t2}{P}\,\htmlClass{t3}{L} \;+\; 4\,\htmlClass{t4}{n_{\text{layers}}}\,\htmlClass{t3}{L}^2\,\htmlClass{t5}{d}$$
FLOPs
arithmetic for one denoiser pass
\(P\)
parameter count (each weight does one multiply-add per token)
\(L\)
sequence length: image (or video) tokens plus text tokens
\(n_{\text{layers}}\)
number of transformer blocks
\(d\)
model width; the \(L^2 d\) term is \(QK^\top\) plus attention-times-\(V\)

In wordsLinear layers cost proportional to tokens; attention costs proportional to tokens squared. Unlike LLM decoding, every pass processes all tokens at once, so diffusion inference is compute-bound like prefill (Module 08).

Why it mattersIllustrative: a 12B transformer on 4,096 image tokens plus 512 text tokens does about \(2\times12\times10^9\times4608 \approx 1.1\times10^{14}\) FLOPs per pass before attention. At 40% of an H100's ~990 TFLOP/s dense bf16 peak, that's roughly 0.3 s per pass. For video, \(L\) grows by 10–100× and the \(L^2\) term takes over.

Few-step methods

There are two ways to cut \(N_{\text{steps}}\). Better solvers use the same network with smarter numerical integration. DPM-Solver (Lu et al., 2022) and the samplers of Karras et al. (2022) brought good samples down to roughly 10–35 network evaluations. Distillation trains a student network to cover several teacher steps in one:

  • Progressive distillation (Salimans & Ho, 2022): repeatedly train a student to match two teacher steps with one, halving the step count each round.
  • Consistency models (Song, Dhariwal, Chen & Sutskever, 2023): learn a function that maps any point on an ODE trajectory straight to the trajectory's end. Latent consistency models (Luo et al., 2023) apply this to Stable Diffusion for 2–4-step sampling.
  • Adversarial distillation: SDXL Turbo (Sauer et al., 2023) adds a discriminator so 1–4-step samples stay sharp rather than averaged. FLUX.1-schnell was trained with a latent variant of this (LADD, Sauer et al., 2024). This is where the GAN loss lives today.
  • Guidance distillation (Meng et al., 2023): bake the CFG combination into one pass, removing the 2× factor.
$$\htmlClass{t1}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t2}{x_t}, t) = \htmlClass{t1}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t2}{x_{t'}}, t') \;\;\text{for all } t, t' \text{ on one ODE trajectory}, \qquad \htmlClass{t1}{f_\theta}(\htmlClass{t3}{x_{t_{\min}}}, t_{\min}) = \htmlClass{t3}{x_{t_{\min}}}$$
\(f_\theta\)
the consistency model: noisy input and time in, clean sample out
\(x_t, x_{t'}\)
two points on the same probability-flow trajectory (one noisier than the other)
\(x_{t_{\min}}\)
the trajectory's (almost) clean end; at that point the model must be the identity

In wordsWherever you are on a path from noise to an image, the model should name the same destination. Train it by making its outputs at neighbouring points agree; the boundary condition anchors the chain to real data.

Why it mattersA perfect consistency model is a one-step generator. In practice 2–4 steps, alternating "jump to the end, re-noise a little", give much better quality than one.

MLE angle · picking a step count

Quality against steps has a knee, and past it extra steps buy almost nothing. For an undistilled model with a good scheduler, start around 20–30 steps and lower it until you can see the difference in a blind side-by-side. For distilled models, use the step count the model card specifies. Running a 4-step model at 30 steps usually doesn't help and can hurt. Latency then scales linearly with steps, which makes it the easiest knob in the whole stack.

Video: spacetime latents and patches

A video is a stack of images, so why not generate frames one at a time? Because independent frames flicker. Textures crawl, objects change shape, lighting jumps. Temporal consistency needs a model that sees all frames, or at least a long window of them, at once. The approach described in OpenAI's Sora technical report ("Video generation models as world simulators", 2024) and used by published open models such as CogVideoX (Yang et al., 2024) and HunyuanVideo (Kong et al., 2024) has three parts:

  1. A video VAE compresses in time as well as space. A common published setting is 4× temporal and 8×8 spatial compression. Many of these VAEs are causal in time: the first frame is encoded on its own, so the same model can also handle still images, and \(F\) frames become \(1 + (F-1)/4\) latent frames.
  2. The latent video is cut into spacetime patches, small blocks spanning a few latent pixels and one or more latent frames. Each block becomes one token.
  3. A diffusion or flow transformer denoises all tokens jointly with full 3D attention, so every patch can attend to every other patch in every frame. Consistency comes from that shared attention.
$$\htmlClass{t1}{N} = \Big(1 + \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{F}-1}{\htmlClass{t3}{c_t}}\Big)\times\frac{\htmlClass{t4}{H}}{\htmlClass{t5}{c_s}\,\htmlClass{t6}{p}}\times\frac{\htmlClass{t4}{W}}{\htmlClass{t5}{c_s}\,\htmlClass{t6}{p}}$$
\(N\)
tokens the transformer attends over (plus text tokens)
\(F\)
frames = duration × fps (+1 under the causal convention)
\(c_t\)
temporal compression of the VAE (e.g. 4)
\(H, W\)
frame height and width in pixels
\(c_s\)
spatial compression of the VAE (e.g. 8)
\(p\)
spatial patch size in latent pixels (e.g. 2; a temporal patch of 1 is assumed here)

In wordsCount latent frames, count patches per latent frame, multiply. Then remember that attention cost grows with the square of that number.

Why it matters5 s of 720p at 24 fps (121 frames) is 111,600 tokens, against 4,096 for a 1024² image. That is 27× the tokens, roughly 27× the linear-layer FLOPs, and \((111{,}600/4{,}096)^2 \approx 740\times\) the attention FLOPs, for every one of maybe 50 steps.

Several factors stack to make video expensive. Token counts grow with duration × resolution, attention grows with the square of that, every step processes everything, and the training data (long, high-quality, well-captioned clips) is scarce and heavy to store and decode. The usual mitigations: generate at low resolution or frame rate and upsample with a second model; use windowed or factorised attention (spatial within a frame, temporal across frames) instead of full 3D attention; compress harder in the VAE; distil to fewer steps; and for long videos, generate in overlapping chunks, each conditioned on the end of the previous one.

What's public and what isn't

The Sora report describes the general recipe (video compression network, spacetime patches, a diffusion transformer, training on variable durations and resolutions) but not its compression factors, model size, or data. The specific numbers in this section come from published open models and are illustrative. Closed systems may differ in every detail.

Autoregressive and masked image generation

The other way to generate images is to make them look like text. A VQ-VAE (van den Oord et al., 2017) is an autoencoder whose latent vectors are snapped to the nearest entry of a learned codebook. Each image patch then becomes an integer, the index of its codebook entry, and an image becomes a sequence of tokens that a transformer can model with ordinary next-token prediction.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{k^\ast} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{k}\,\big\lVert \htmlClass{t2}{z_e} - \htmlClass{t3}{e_k}\big\rVert^2, \qquad \htmlClass{t4}{z_q} = \htmlClass{t3}{e_{k^\ast}}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{VQ}} = \lVert \mathrm{sg}[\htmlClass{t2}{z_e}] - \htmlClass{t4}{z_q}\rVert^2 + \htmlClass{t5}{\beta}\,\lVert \htmlClass{t2}{z_e} - \mathrm{sg}[\htmlClass{t4}{z_q}]\rVert^2$$
\(k^\ast\)
the token: index of the closest codebook vector
\(z_e\)
encoder output for one patch (a continuous vector)
\(e_k\)
codebook entries, learned (\(K\) of them, e.g. 1,024 or 16,384)
\(z_q\)
the quantized vector passed to the decoder
\(\beta\)
commitment weight (0.25 in the paper): keeps encoder outputs near their codes

In wordsReplace each patch vector with its nearest neighbour from a fixed menu, and keep only the menu index. \(\mathrm{sg}\) means stop-gradient: the first term moves codes toward encoder outputs, and the second keeps the encoder from drifting away from the codes. Argmin has no gradient, so the decoder's gradient is copied straight to the encoder (the straight-through trick from Module 09).

Why it mattersEach token carries \(\log_2 K\) bits: 10 bits for \(K=1024\). A 256×256 image at 16× downsampling is 16×16 = 256 tokens, so 2,560 bits describe the whole image, with the decoder hallucinating plausible detail.

VQGAN (Esser, Rombach & Ommer, 2021) made the tokens good enough to use by adding perceptual and adversarial losses to the autoencoder. Then a transformer predicts tokens in raster order: DALL·E (Ramesh et al., 2021) and Parti (Yu et al., 2022) generated images exactly this way. The cost is serial decoding: 256 to 1,024 forward passes per image, one per token.

MaskGIT (Chang et al., 2022) generates tokens in parallel instead. A bidirectional transformer is trained BERT-style to fill in randomly masked tokens. At sampling time it starts with every token masked, predicts all of them, keeps the most confident fraction, re-masks the rest, and repeats. The fraction still masked follows a cosine schedule, \(\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot\tfrac{i}{K})\) after iteration \(i\) of \(K\), and 8 iterations sufficed for 256 tokens. Muse (Chang et al., 2023) scaled the idea to text-to-image.

Autoregression is back, in a different place

Natively multimodal models put image tokens into the same sequence as text. Meta's Chameleon (2024) and BAAI's Emu3 (2024) published models that generate images by next-token prediction over VQ tokens. OpenAI described GPT-4o's image generation (2025) as autoregressive rather than diffusion-based, without publishing the architecture. Hybrids exist too: Transfusion (Zhou et al., 2024) trains one transformer with a next-token loss on text and a diffusion loss on image patches. Which approach wins for which product is still an open question. The likely advantage of the autoregressive route is tight integration with a language model's world knowledge and instruction following, not raw image quality.

Audio and music generation

Audio is a 1D signal at very high rate: 24,000 samples per second at 24 kHz. Module 14 covered how models read audio through spectrograms and neural codecs. Generation uses the same representations in reverse, through three pipelines.

Classic TTS: text → spectrogram → vocoder

An acoustic model such as Tacotron 2 (Shen et al., 2018) maps text to a mel spectrogram, and a neural vocoder (WaveNet; HiFi-GAN, Kong et al., 2020) turns the spectrogram into a waveform. The vocoder exists because spectrograms throw away phase, which must be re-invented to produce audio. This pipeline gives clean speech, but it's hard to clone a new voice from a short sample.

Codec language models

A neural codec such as SoundStream (Zeghidour et al., 2021) or EnCodec (Défossez et al., 2022) compresses audio into a short sequence of frames and quantizes each frame with residual vector quantization (RVQ): a stack of codebooks, each encoding what the previous ones missed.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{r_0} = z, \qquad \htmlClass{t2}{k_m} = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_k \big\lVert \htmlClass{t1}{r_{m-1}} - \htmlClass{t3}{e^{(m)}_k}\big\rVert, \qquad \htmlClass{t1}{r_m} = \htmlClass{t1}{r_{m-1}} - \htmlClass{t3}{e^{(m)}_{\htmlClass{t2}{k_m}}}, \qquad \hat z = \sum_{m=1}^{\htmlClass{t4}{M}} \htmlClass{t3}{e^{(m)}_{\htmlClass{t2}{k_m}}}$$
\(r_m\)
residual: the part of the frame vector not yet explained after \(m\) codebooks
\(k_m\)
the token from codebook \(m\) for this frame
\(e^{(m)}_k\)
entry \(k\) of codebook \(m\); each level has its own codebook
\(M\)
number of codebooks used; more codebooks means higher bitrate and fidelity

In wordsQuantize coarsely, measure what's left over, quantize the leftover, and repeat. Early codebooks capture the gist (who is speaking, which phoneme); later ones add acoustic detail.

Why it mattersEnCodec at 24 kHz produces 75 frames per second. With 8 codebooks of 1,024 entries that's 75 × 8 = 600 tokens per second, and 75 × 8 × 10 bits = 6 kbps. A 10-second clip is 6,000 tokens: long, but manageable for a transformer.

With audio as tokens, generation becomes language modelling. AudioLM (Borsos et al., 2022) first predicts coarse "semantic" tokens from a self-supervised speech model, then the codec tokens that realise them. VALL-E (Wang et al., 2023) treats text-to-speech as language modelling over EnCodec tokens, conditioned on phonemes plus a 3-second recording of the target speaker, which gives zero-shot voice cloning. MusicGen (Copet et al., 2023) predicts several codebooks per step with a "delay" interleaving pattern, so it doesn't need one pass per codebook level.

Diffusion and flow for audio

Everything from the image sections transfers to audio. AudioLDM (Liu et al., 2023) and Stable Audio (Evans et al., 2024) run latent diffusion on compressed audio or spectrogram latents, with Stable Audio adding timing conditioning so you can ask for a specific duration. Voicebox (Le et al., 2023) and F5-TTS (Chen et al., 2024) use flow matching for speech. Diffusion and flow models produce a whole clip in parallel; codec language models stream naturally, token by token. That streaming property is why real-time voice systems such as Moshi (Défossez et al., 2024) model codec tokens directly.

Evaluating generative media

There is no single right answer to compare against, so evaluation asks two separate questions. Do the samples look like real data? And do they match the prompt? The most cited metric for the first question is FID (Heusel et al., 2017).

$$\mathrm{FID} = \big\lVert \htmlClass{t1}{\mu_r} - \htmlClass{t2}{\mu_g}\big\rVert^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\Big(\htmlClass{t3}{\Sigma_r} + \htmlClass{t4}{\Sigma_g} - 2\big(\htmlClass{t3}{\Sigma_r}\htmlClass{t4}{\Sigma_g}\big)^{1/2}\Big)$$
\(\mu_r\)
mean of Inception-v3 features (2,048-dim) over real images
\(\mu_g\)
mean of the same features over generated images
\(\Sigma_r\)
covariance of real-image features
\(\Sigma_g\)
covariance of generated-image features

In wordsEmbed both image sets with an ImageNet classifier, fit a Gaussian to each cloud of embeddings, and measure the distance between the Gaussians. The first term compares their centres, the second their shapes and spreads. Lower is better.

Why it mattersIt catches both bad quality (the generated cloud shifts) and low diversity (it shrinks). It is also biased at small sample sizes, so compare only at a fixed count (often 50,000). It depends on resizing and compression details, which tools like clean-fid (Parmar et al., 2022) pin down. And it sees images through ImageNet features that say nothing about the prompt. A model can improve FID while getting worse at following text.

MetricMeasuresBlind spot
FIDdistribution match to real imagesignores the prompt; sample-size bias; ImageNet-centric features
CLIP score (Hessel et al., 2021)cosine similarity between CLIP image and text embeddingsCLIP is weak at counting, spatial relations, and attribute binding ("red cube on a blue sphere")
GenEval (Ghosh et al., 2023), T2I-CompBenchcompositional correctness checked with detectors or VQA modelslimited to what the checker can detect
Learned preference models (PickScore, ImageReward, HPS)predicted human preferencecan be gamed when you optimise against them directly

Pitfall · optimising the judge

Fine-tuning a generator to maximise a learned preference score or CLIP score (via RL or reward backprop) works, and then overshoots. You get oversaturated, over-detailed images that score well and look "AI". It's the same reward hacking as LLM RLHF (Module 10). Keep a held-out human evaluation, or at least a second, independent metric.

Provenance and watermarking

Two complementary approaches exist. C2PA content credentials attach a cryptographically signed manifest to a file recording how it was made. They are easy to verify and easy to strip: a screenshot or re-encode drops the metadata. Invisible watermarks such as Google DeepMind's SynthID embed a signal in the pixels or audio themselves. They're designed to survive common edits like cropping, compression, and filters, but not a determined adversary. After-the-fact "AI detector" classifiers are unreliable on new generators. Treat all of these as evidence, not proof.

2. A minimal DDPM on 2D toy data

The same six-blob ring as the widgets. A small MLP stands in for the U-Net. On a laptop CPU it trains in a few minutes.


import math, torch, torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
dev = "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu"
CENTERS = torch.tensor([[1.7 * math.cos(math.pi / 2 + k * math.pi / 3),
                         1.7 * math.sin(math.pi / 2 + k * math.pi / 3)] for k in range(6)])

def sample_data(n):                                   # six Gaussian blobs on a ring
    return CENTERS[torch.randint(0, 6, (n,))] + 0.28 * torch.randn(n, 2)

def on_target(x):                                     # share of points within 2.5 sigma of a centre
    return (torch.cdist(x, CENTERS).min(1).values < 2.5 * 0.28).float().mean().item()

T = 1000
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)                 # Ho et al. (2020) linear schedule
alphas = 1 - betas
abar = torch.cumprod(alphas, 0)

class Denoiser(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, h=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2 + 16, h), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(h, h), nn.SiLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(h, h), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(h, 2))
    def forward(self, x, t):                          # t in [0, T), int or float, shape (n,)
        freqs = torch.exp(torch.linspace(0, math.log(1000), 8, device=x.device))
        ang = t[:, None].float() / T * freqs[None]    # sinusoidal timestep features
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, torch.sin(ang), torch.cos(ang)], 1))

model = Denoiser().to(dev)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
ab = abar.to(dev)
for step in range(20_000):
    x0 = sample_data(512).to(dev)
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (512,), device=dev)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    a = ab[t][:, None]
    xt = a.sqrt() * x0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * eps         # forward process in one jump
    loss = ((model(xt, t) - eps) ** 2).mean()         # predict the noise
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 2000 == 0:
        print(step, round(loss.item(), 4))

@torch.no_grad()
def ddpm_sample(n):
    x = torch.randn(n, 2, device=dev)
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        eps = model(x, torch.full((n,), t, device=dev))
        a, abt, b = alphas[t].item(), abar[t].item(), betas[t].item()
        x = (x - b / math.sqrt(1 - abt) * eps) / math.sqrt(a)   # posterior mean
        if t > 0:
            x = x + math.sqrt(b) * torch.randn_like(x)          # fresh noise
    return x.cpu()

print("DDPM on-target:", on_target(ddpm_sample(2000)), " true data:", on_target(sample_data(2000)))

3. Flow matching in thirty lines

Reuse Denoiser, sample_data, and on_target from above. Only the training target and the sampler change.


fm = Denoiser().to(dev)                               # same MLP, now predicts velocity
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(fm.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for step in range(20_000):
    x0 = sample_data(512).to(dev)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    t = torch.rand(512, device=dev)                   # t=0 data, t=1 noise
    xt = (1 - t[:, None]) * x0 + t[:, None] * eps     # straight line between them
    v_target = eps - x0                               # constant velocity along the line
    loss = ((fm(xt, t * T) - v_target) ** 2).mean()   # feed t scaled to the embedding's range
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

@torch.no_grad()
def euler_sample(n, steps):
    x = torch.randn(n, 2, device=dev)                 # start at t = 1
    ts = torch.linspace(1.0, 0.0, steps + 1)
    for i in range(steps):
        t, t_next = ts[i].item(), ts[i + 1].item()
        v = fm(x, torch.full((n,), t * T, device=dev))
        x = x + (t_next - t) * v                      # t_next < t: move toward data
    return x.cpu()

for steps in (1, 2, 4, 8, 32):
    print(f"{steps:2d} Euler steps -> on-target {on_target(euler_sample(2000, steps)):.2f}")

At a step where \(\bar\alpha_t = 0.64\), a pixel has clean value \(x_0 = 1.0\) and the sampled noise is \(\varepsilon = -0.5\). What are \(x_t\) and the SNR?

  1. \(x_t = 0.32\), SNR = 0.64
  2. \(x_t = 1.1\), SNR \(\approx\) 1.78
  3. \(x_t = 0.5\), SNR \(\approx\) 1.78
  4. \(x_t = 0.46\), SNR \(\approx\) 0.56
\(x_t = \sqrt{0.64}\cdot1.0 + \sqrt{0.36}\cdot(-0.5) = 0.8 - 0.3 = 0.5\). The coefficients are square roots of the variances, which is why 0.64 and 0.36 don't appear directly. SNR = 0.64/0.36 \(\approx\) 1.78, about 2.5 dB: more signal than noise. 1.1 uses \(+0.5\) for the noise; 0.46 skips the square roots.

Raising the classifier-free guidance scale from 1 to 7 makes images match the prompt better but look more alike. Why?

  1. Guidance samples from roughly \(p(x)\,p(c\mid x)^{w}\), which concentrates mass on images an implicit classifier is most sure about and empties ambiguous modes
  2. Guidance adds extra noise, which averages samples together
  3. Guidance halves the number of sampling steps
  4. The unconditional branch is dropped, so the model forgets rare images
The guided score is \(\nabla\log p(x) + w\,\nabla\log p(c\mid x)\). For \(w \gt 1\) the classifier term is exaggerated, so samples move toward regions where \(c\) is unmistakable. In the CFG widget, the middle blob that overlaps the other class empties first. Fewer modes means less diversity, and overshooting means oversaturation.

A VAE with 8× spatial downsampling and 16 latent channels encodes a 1024×1024 RGB image. By what factor does it reduce the count of numbers the denoiser works on?

  1. 48×
  2. 64×
  3. 192×
  4. 12×
Pixels: \(1024\cdot1024\cdot3 = 3{,}145{,}728\). Latent: \(128\cdot128\cdot16 = 262{,}144\). Ratio 12. The 48× figure is the 4-channel Stable Diffusion 1.x VAE. Going from 4 to 16 channels trades compression for detail. 64× counts only the spatial reduction and ignores channels.

Flow matching with \(x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\varepsilon\). For \(x_0=(1,3)\) and \(\varepsilon=(-1,1)\), what is the velocity target, and where is the point at \(t=0.5\)?

  1. \(v=(2,2)\); \(x_{0.5}=(0,2)\)
  2. \(v=(-2,-2)\); \(x_{0.5}=(0,2)\)
  3. \(v=(-2,-2)\); \(x_{0.5}=(0.5,1.5)\)
  4. It depends on \(t\), so there's no single target
\(v = \varepsilon - x_0 = (-2,-2)\), constant along the straight line. \(x_{0.5} = 0.5\cdot(1,3) + 0.5\cdot(-1,1) = (0,2)\). \((2,2)\) is \(x_0-\varepsilon\), the velocity under the opposite time convention, which is a common porting bug.

A denoiser pass takes 80 ms. Model A uses 30 steps with classifier-free guidance; model B is distilled to 4 steps with no CFG. Ignoring text-encoder and VAE time, what's A's latency and how much faster is B?

  1. 2.4 s; 7.5×
  2. 4.8 s; 7.5×
  3. 4.8 s; 15×
  4. 9.6 s; 30×
A: 30 × 2 × 0.08 = 4.8 s, since CFG means two passes per step. B: 4 × 1 × 0.08 = 0.32 s. 4.8 / 0.32 = 15. Forgetting the CFG factor gives 2.4 s and 7.5×.

A video model's causal VAE compresses 4× in time and 8×8 in space; the transformer uses 2×2 patches. How many tokens is a 121-frame 1280×720 clip?

  1. ~14,400
  2. ~111,600
  3. ~446,400
  4. ~3.6 million
Latent frames \(1 + 120/4 = 31\). Per latent frame \((720/16)\cdot(1280/16) = 45\cdot80 = 3{,}600\). Total 111,600. Against a 4,096-token 1024² image that's 27× the tokens and about \(27^2\approx 740\times\) the attention cost, per step. 446,400 forgets the patch size; 14,400 forgets the frames.

With a perfect denoiser, a 1-step DDIM sample from pure noise lands at the mean of the dataset (the empty centre of the ring). Why?

  1. The denoiser was never trained at \(t=T\)
  2. DDIM is deterministic, so all samples must be identical
  3. \(\bar\alpha_T = 1\), so no noise is removed
  4. The MSE-optimal prediction is \(\mathbb{E}[x_0\mid x_T]\), and pure noise carries no information about \(x_0\), so that's the overall mean
Squared-error training makes the network output a conditional average. At \(\bar\alpha_T\approx0\), \(x_T\) is independent of the image, so the average is the same for every input: the dataset mean, or a grey blur for images. Determinism alone doesn't force identical outputs, since different noise could map to different points with more steps. Distilled few-step models are trained specifically to escape this.
