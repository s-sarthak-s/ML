Module 11 · Adapting models

Retrieval-augmented generation: giving the model open-book access

A model trained last year has never seen your wiki, your tickets, or this morning's policy change. RAG fixes that by fetching the right paragraphs at question time and putting them in the prompt. The "fetching" part is a search-engine problem with fifty years of history, and it is where most RAG systems succeed or fail.

Why retrieval: the model knows a lot, but not your stuff

A pretrained model is a compressed snapshot of its training data. That snapshot has four gaps that matter in practice:

  1. Knowledge cutoff. Nothing after the training data was collected exists for the model. Ask about last week's release and it will either refuse or, worse, extrapolate confidently.
  2. Private data. Your runbooks, contracts, code, and customer tickets were never in the training set, and you wouldn't want them baked into weights that anyone with the model could query.
  3. Provenance. A model's parametric memory can't tell you where it learned something. For support, legal, medical, or internal-policy answers, "according to refund-policy.md, section 2" is the product.
  4. Hallucination on the long tail. Models are reliable on facts repeated thousands of times in training and shaky on facts seen once. Your company's error code ERR-4012 is very long tail.
Definition · Retrieval-augmented generation

At query time, retrieve passages relevant to the question from an external corpus, insert them into the prompt, and have the model generate an answer grounded in them. The term comes from Lewis et al. (2020), who trained the retriever and generator jointly; REALM (Guu et al., 2020) did retrieval during pretraining. Today "RAG" usually means the looser engineering pattern: an off-the-shelf retriever feeding an off-the-shelf LLM through the prompt.

Grounding reduces hallucination but doesn't eliminate it. The model can still ignore the context, blend it with its priors, or cite a passage that doesn't say what it claims. That's why later sections measure faithfulness separately from retrieval quality.

Intuition

Fine-tuning is studying for an exam: it changes how the student thinks and writes, but facts crammed the night before are fuzzy. Long context is bringing the entire library into the exam room. RAG is the open-book exam with a good index: you look up the three pages you need and quote them.

Think first: your whole knowledge base is 40 pages that change monthly. Do you need RAG?
Probably not. 40 pages is roughly 25–30K tokens. Put all of it in the system prompt, turn on prompt caching so you don't pay full price for the static prefix on every call, and skip the retrieval stack entirely. RAG earns its complexity when the corpus is too big for the window, changes faster than you want to rebuild prompts, needs per-user permissions, or when paying for 30K tokens per query is too expensive at your traffic.

The full pipeline

Every RAG system has two halves that run on different clocks. The ingest path runs offline, in batch or on change events, and turns raw documents into searchable indexes. The query path runs per request, under a latency budget, and turns a question into a grounded answer.

Walk through it once with a concrete question: "Why did my refund fail with ERR-4012?"

  1. Ingest pulled billing/refunds.md from the wiki, parsed it, split it into chunks of a few hundred tokens, embedded each chunk into a 384- to 3072-dimensional vector, and wrote three things: the vector into an ANN index, the terms into a BM25 inverted index, and the text plus metadata (source URL, heading path, last-modified date, access groups) into a document store.
  2. Rewrite turns a chat follow-up like "what about for bank transfers?" into a standalone query, and may extract filters ("product = billing").
  3. Retrieve runs a dense search (embedding similarity) and a sparse search (BM25) in parallel, each returning its top 50 or so.
  4. Fuse merges the two ranked lists with Reciprocal Rank Fusion.
  5. Rerank scores the merged candidates with a slower, more accurate cross-encoder and keeps the best 5–10.
  6. Assemble deduplicates, orders, trims to a token budget, and numbers each chunk so the model can cite it.
  7. Generate calls the LLM with instructions to answer only from the numbered sources.
  8. Cite and log: check that every [n] points to a real source, map it to a URL, and record everything.
MLE angle · debug retrieval first

When a RAG answer is wrong, look at what was retrieved before you touch the prompt. If the passage containing the answer never reached the context window, no prompt engineering can fix it. Make the retrieved chunk IDs and scores visible in your logs and debug UI from day one; most teams that skip this spend weeks tuning prompts for what turns out to be a parsing bug.

Cleaning and metadata

After parsing: remove exact duplicates (hash the normalized text) and near-duplicates (MinHash or embedding similarity above about 0.95), because duplicates waste the top-k slots on the same fact. Then attach metadata to every chunk. You'll want it later for filtering, citations, freshness, and access control:

  • source (URL or path), title, heading_path ("Billing › Refunds › Error codes"), page
  • updated_at, version, and a content_hash so re-ingest can skip unchanged documents
  • acl: the groups allowed to see it, copied from the source system
  • domain fields you'll filter on: product, region, language, document type
Pitfall · never evaluating the parser

Teams evaluate the embedding model for days and never open the parsed text. Spend ten minutes reading 20 random parsed documents side by side with the originals before building anything else. If a human can't answer the question from the parsed text, no retriever will help.

Chunking strategies

You can't embed a 60-page document as one vector and expect it to match a question about page 37. There are three reasons to split documents into chunks:

  1. Embedding fidelity. An embedding is one point summarizing the whole input. The longer and more varied the text, the more that point becomes a blurry average of many topics. Many embedding models also truncate input silently at 512 tokens.
  2. Context budget. You want the five most relevant paragraphs in the prompt, not five whole documents.
  3. Citation granularity. "See section 2.3" is useful. "See the employee handbook" is not.

But chunks that are too small lose context. "It must be filed within 7 days" is useless if the chunk doesn't say what "it" is. Chunking trades precision of matching against completeness of context.

The four main strategies

Fixed-size + overlap

Cut every s tokens

Slide a window of size \(s\) with step \(s-o\). Trivial to implement, predictable sizes, and it cuts sentences and even words in half. The overlap \(o\) makes sure a fact that straddles a boundary appears whole in at least one chunk.

Recursive by separators

Split on the biggest natural break that fits

Try paragraphs first; if a piece is still too big, split it on lines, then sentences, then words. Then greedily pack pieces into chunks of at most \(s\). This is what LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter does, and it's a strong default.

Structure-aware

Respect the document's own outline

Split at markdown or HTML headings, slide boundaries, or code functions first, and never let a chunk cross a section boundary. Prepend the heading path ("Refunds › ERR-4012") to each chunk so it carries its context with it.

Semantic

Split where the topic changes

Embed each sentence, and start a new chunk where the similarity between neighbouring sentences drops below a threshold. It adapts to the content but costs an embedding per sentence, and its gains over a good recursive splitter are inconsistent. Measure before adopting it.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{n_{\text{chunks}}} = \left\lceil \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{L} - \htmlClass{t4}{o}}{\htmlClass{t3}{s} - \htmlClass{t4}{o}} \right\rceil, \qquad \text{stored text} \approx \htmlClass{t2}{L}\cdot\frac{\htmlClass{t3}{s}}{\htmlClass{t3}{s} - \htmlClass{t4}{o}}$$
\(n_{\text{chunks}}\)
number of chunks, so the number of embeddings to compute and store
\(L\)
document length in tokens (or characters; just be consistent)
\(s\)

\(o\)

In wordsEach new chunk advances by \(s-o\), so overlap multiplies storage and embedding work by \(s/(s-o)\). A 100,000-token handbook with \(s=512, o=64\) gives \(\lceil 99{,}936/448\rceil = 224\) chunks and about 14% duplicated text.

Why it mattersOverlap is cheap insurance at 10–20%. At 50% you've doubled your index and your top-k results start returning the same sentence twice.

Choosing a size

A common starting point is 256–512 tokens with 10–20% overlap, split recursively and respecting headings. Treat that as a prior, not an answer. The right size depends on your questions: factoid lookups ("what's the rate limit?") favour small chunks, while "explain how refunds interact with disputes" favours larger ones. Sweep two or three sizes against your golden set (section 11.12) and keep the winner. Two techniques let you avoid choosing at all: parent-document retrieval (search small, return big; section 11.10) and late chunking (Günther et al., 2024), which embeds the whole document with a long-context model first and pools token embeddings per chunk afterwards, so each chunk's vector has seen its surroundings.

Pitfall · characters are not tokens

Chunk sizes in libraries are often in characters, while model limits are in tokens. English prose averages roughly 4 characters per token; code, URLs, and many non-English languages are much denser in tokens. Measure with the embedding model's own tokenizer, and check that your longest chunk fits its maximum sequence length. Anything past the limit is dropped from the embedding without warning.

Embeddings for retrieval

Module 05 introduced token embeddings: one vector per token, inside the model. Retrieval needs one vector per passage, such that passages answering a question land near that question. The standard architecture is the bi-encoder (Sentence-BERT, Reimers & Gurevych, 2019; DPR, Karpukhin et al., 2020): a transformer encoder reads the text, its output token vectors are pooled (mean or the first token) into one vector, and the vector is normalized to unit length.

How embedding models are trained

Embedding models learn from (query, relevant passage) pairs: search logs, question–answer sites, title–body pairs, and increasingly LLM-generated synthetic queries. The loss asks the model to pick the right passage out of a lineup:

$$\htmlClass{t1}{\mathcal{L}} = -\frac{1}{\htmlClass{t6}{B}}\sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t6}{B}} \log \frac{\exp\!\big(\text{sim}(\htmlClass{t2}{q_i}, \htmlClass{t3}{d_i^{+}})/\htmlClass{t4}{\tau}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{\htmlClass{t6}{B}} \exp\!\big(\text{sim}(\htmlClass{t2}{q_i}, \htmlClass{t5}{d_j})/\htmlClass{t4}{\tau}\big)}$$
\(\mathcal{L}\)
loss averaged over the batch; minimizing it pulls true pairs together and pushes others apart
\(q_i\)
embedding of the \(i\)-th query
\(d_i^{+}\)
embedding of the passage that actually answers \(q_i\) (the positive)
\(\tau\)
temperature. Cosine similarities live in [−1, 1]; dividing by a small \(\tau\) (often around 0.01–0.05) stretches them so the softmax can become confident. Too large and every passage looks equally likely; too small and training becomes unstable and overfits to the hardest negatives
\(d_j\)
every passage in the batch. For \(j \neq i\) these are in-batch negatives: other queries' positives, reused for free
\(B\)
batch size; each query sees \(B-1\) negatives

In wordsFor each query, do a \(B\)-way classification: "which of these passages is mine?" It's ordinary cross-entropy (Module 03) where the logits are similarities divided by temperature. The name comes from van den Oord et al. (2018).

Why it mattersBigger batches give more negatives, which makes the task harder and the embeddings sharper. That's why embedding models train with batch sizes in the thousands. Random negatives get easy quickly, so pipelines add hard negatives mined with BM25 or an earlier model: passages that share words with the query but don't answer it. In sentence-transformers this loss is MultipleNegativesRankingLoss.

Measuring similarity

$$\cos(\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{q}}, \htmlClass{t2}{\mathbf{d}}) = \frac{\htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{q}} \cdot \htmlClass{t2}{\mathbf{d}}}{\lVert \htmlClass{t1}{\mathbf{q}} \rVert \, \lVert \htmlClass{t2}{\mathbf{d}} \rVert}, \qquad \lVert \htmlClass{t3}{\hat{\mathbf{q}}} - \htmlClass{t3}{\hat{\mathbf{d}}} \rVert^2 = 2 - 2\, \htmlClass{t3}{\hat{\mathbf{q}}} \cdot \htmlClass{t3}{\hat{\mathbf{d}}}$$
\(\mathbf{q}\)
query embedding, a vector of \(d\) floats
\(\mathbf{d}\)
passage embedding, same dimension
\(\hat{\mathbf{q}}, \hat{\mathbf{d}}\)
the same vectors scaled to unit length (\(\mathbf{v}/\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert\))

In wordsCosine measures the angle between vectors and ignores their lengths. Once vectors are unit length, cosine is just the dot product, and squared Euclidean distance is a decreasing function of it. All three give the same ranking.

Why it mattersNormalize once at index time and you can use the fastest primitive everywhere: an inner-product index (faiss.IndexFlatIP) on normalized vectors is cosine search. Forget to normalize and longer or more "generic" passages with larger norms quietly win more often.

Dimension, storage, and Matryoshka truncation

Embedding models output anywhere from 384 dimensions (small BERT-sized models) to 3,072 (OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large) or more. Storage is simple arithmetic: 1 million chunks × 1,024 dims × 4 bytes = 4.1 GB in float32, 2 GB in float16, 1 GB in int8, and 128 MB as binary (1 bit per dim, then re-score the top few hundred with full vectors).

Matryoshka Representation Learning (Kusupati et al., 2022) trains the model so that the first \(m\) dimensions are themselves a good embedding, by applying the loss at several prefix lengths (e.g. 64, 128, 256, … , full). You can then truncate vectors to trade quality for memory and speed without re-embedding. OpenAI exposes this as the dimensions parameter, and reported that text-embedding-3-large shortened to 256 dims still beat their previous 1,536-dim model on the MTEB benchmark. Several open models (e.g. Nomic Embed v1.5) support it too.

Pitfall · truncate, then renormalize

A truncated Matryoshka vector is no longer unit length. Renormalize after truncation or your "cosine" index is computing something else. And truncation only works for models trained this way; chopping a normal embedding to a third of its dimensions degrades it badly.

Choosing an embedding model

  • Start from the retrieval tab of the MTEB leaderboard (Muennighoff et al., 2022), filtered to models you can afford to run. Then test the top three on your golden set; leaderboard ranks routinely reshuffle on domain data.
  • Use the model's query/passage conventions. E5 models expect "query: " and "passage: " prefixes; BGE v1.5 recommends an instruction prefix on queries. Skipping them costs recall.
  • Check maximum sequence length against your chunk size.
  • Vectors from different models (or different versions of one model) are not comparable. Changing the model means re-embedding the entire corpus, so plan for it with a versioned index.
  • If you have a few thousand in-domain (query, passage) pairs, fine-tuning a small embedding model with the InfoNCE loss above is one of the highest-return changes in RAG.

Vector search: exact and approximate

Given a query vector, find the \(k\) stored vectors with the highest similarity. The obvious way is to compare against all of them.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{C_{\text{flat}}} = \htmlClass{t2}{N} \cdot \htmlClass{t3}{d}\ \text{multiply-adds per query}, \qquad \htmlClass{t4}{M_{\text{flat}}} = \htmlClass{t2}{N} \cdot \htmlClass{t3}{d} \cdot \htmlClass{t5}{4}\ \text{bytes}$$
\(C_{\text{flat}}\)
compute per query: one dot product with every stored vector, so \(O(N d)\)
\(N\)

\(d\)
embedding dimension
\(M_{\text{flat}}\)

4

In wordsBrute force touches every byte of the index on every query. 10 million chunks × 768 dims is 7.7 billion multiply-adds and 30.7 GB of reads. At CPU memory bandwidth of 50–100 GB/s that's 0.3–0.6 s per query before anything else happens; it's the same bandwidth wall as decoding in Module 08.

Why it mattersUp to a few hundred thousand vectors, exact search is fast enough and has perfect recall, so use it and skip the tuning. Past a few million, you need an approximate index.

Approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) indexes avoid looking at most vectors. They give up a little recall (some true top-k neighbours get missed) for orders-of-magnitude speedups. You measure that loss as ANN recall@k: the fraction of the exact top-k that the index returns. Three ideas cover nearly every index you'll meet: partition the space (IVF), navigate a graph (HNSW), and compress the vectors (PQ).

IVF: partition into cells, search only the nearby ones

An inverted-file index (Jégou et al., 2011, popularized by FAISS, Johnson et al., 2017) runs k-means on the vectors to get \(n_{\text{list}}\) centroids. Each vector is filed under its nearest centroid, which carves the space into Voronoi cells, each with a list of its members. At query time, compare the query with all centroids, then scan only the \(n_{\text{probe}}\) closest lists.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{C_{\text{IVF}}} \approx \htmlClass{t2}{n_{\text{list}}} \cdot \htmlClass{t5}{d} \;+\; \htmlClass{t3}{n_{\text{probe}}} \cdot \frac{\htmlClass{t4}{N}}{\htmlClass{t2}{n_{\text{list}}}} \cdot \htmlClass{t5}{d}$$
\(C_{\text{IVF}}\)
multiply-adds per query
\(n_{\text{list}}\)
number of cells (k-means centroids). FAISS's guidelines suggest roughly \(4\sqrt{N}\) to \(16\sqrt{N}\)
\(n_{\text{probe}}\)
cells scanned per query: the recall/latency dial you tune at query time
\(N\)
total vectors; \(N/n_{\text{list}}\) is the average list length (assuming balanced cells)
\(d\)

In wordsPay once to find the right neighbourhoods, then pay for each neighbourhood you search. With \(N=10\text{M}\), \(n_{\text{list}}=16{,}384\), \(n_{\text{probe}}=32\): 12.6M + 15M ≈ 28M multiply-adds instead of 7.7 billion, about 280× less work.

Why it mattersCost grows linearly with \(n_{\text{probe}}\); recall rises quickly at first and then flattens. Tune \(n_{\text{probe}}\) on held-out queries to the knee of that curve.

HNSW: a navigable small-world graph

Hierarchical Navigable Small World graphs (Malkov & Yashunin, 2016) are the default index in most vector databases. Each vector is a node linked to about \(M\) of its near neighbours. Search is greedy: start somewhere, repeatedly hop to whichever neighbour is closest to the query, stop when no neighbour is closer. A flat graph like that needs many short hops to cross the space, so HNSW adds layers, like express lanes. Every node is in layer 0; each node also appears in higher layers with exponentially decreasing probability. Search enters at the sparse top layer, makes a few long hops, drops down a layer, and repeats.

Three parameters control it:

  • M: links per node, typically 16–64. More links give better recall and more memory; in FAISS's layout the graph adds roughly \(2M \times 4\) bytes per vector on top of the vectors themselves.
  • efConstruction: beam width while building, typically 100–500. Higher builds a better graph, more slowly.
  • efSearch: beam width at query time, typically 50–500, and at least \(k\). This is HNSW's \(n_{\text{probe}}\): the main recall/latency knob.

Search cost grows roughly logarithmically with \(N\), and recall is excellent (often above 0.95 at sensible settings). The downsides: it holds full vectors plus the graph in RAM, builds slowly at very large scale, and deletions are awkward (most implementations mark deleted nodes and rebuild periodically).

Product quantization: compress the vectors

At 100 million vectors, even the raw float32 data (307 GB at 768 dims) won't fit in RAM. Product quantization (Jégou, Douze & Schmid, 2011) splits each vector into \(m\) sub-vectors and replaces each sub-vector with the ID of its nearest centroid from a small learned codebook. It's the same idea as the group-wise quantization in Module 09, except each group is snapped to a learned codebook entry rather than a uniform grid.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{M_{\text{PQ}}} = \frac{\htmlClass{t2}{N} \cdot \htmlClass{t3}{m} \cdot \log_2 \htmlClass{t4}{k}}{8}\ \text{bytes} \qquad \text{vs} \qquad \htmlClass{t5}{M_{\text{flat}}} = \htmlClass{t2}{N} \cdot \htmlClass{t6}{d} \cdot 4\ \text{bytes}$$
\(M_{\text{PQ}}\)

\(N\)

\(m\)

\(k\)

\(M_{\text{flat}}\)

\(d\)

In wordsEach vector shrinks from \(4d\) bytes to \(m\) bytes. 100M vectors at \(d=768\) with \(m=96\), \(k=256\): 9.6 GB instead of 307 GB, a 32× reduction.

Why it mattersCompression costs accuracy, because distances become approximate. The standard fix is to re-rank: fetch a few hundred candidates with PQ distances, then recompute exact distances for those from full vectors kept on disk.

Lexical retrieval with BM25

Before embeddings, search engines matched words. They still do, and for good reason. An inverted index maps each term to the list of documents containing it (its postings), so a query only touches the postings of its own terms. The scoring function almost everyone uses is Okapi BM25, from Robertson and colleagues' work at City University London in the 1990s (see Robertson & Zaragoza, 2009, for the full derivation). It refines TF-IDF with two ideas: repeated terms should help with diminishing returns, and long documents shouldn't win just by containing more words.

$$\text{score}(\htmlClass{t1}{D}, \htmlClass{t2}{Q}) = \sum_{\htmlClass{t3}{t} \in \htmlClass{t2}{Q}} \text{IDF}(\htmlClass{t3}{t}) \cdot \frac{\htmlClass{t4}{f_{t,D}} \, (\htmlClass{t5}{k_1} + 1)}{\htmlClass{t4}{f_{t,D}} + \htmlClass{t5}{k_1} \left(1 - \htmlClass{t6}{b} + \htmlClass{t6}{b} \cdot \dfrac{\htmlClass{t7}{\lvert D \rvert}}{\htmlClass{t7}{\text{avgdl}}}\right)}$$
\(D\)
the document (chunk) being scored
\(Q\)
the query, after the same tokenization as the documents
\(t\)
each query term; terms not in \(D\) contribute 0
\(f_{t,D}\)
term frequency: how many times \(t\) occurs in \(D\)
\(k_1\)
saturation, typically 1.2–2.0. The term-frequency part rises from 1 toward a ceiling of \(k_1+1\). With \(k_1=0\) only presence counts; large \(k_1\) behaves closer to raw counts
\(b\)
length normalization, 0 to 1, typically 0.75. At \(b=1\), a document twice the average length needs twice the occurrences for the same credit; at \(b=0\), length is ignored
\(\lvert D\rvert / \text{avgdl}\)
document length relative to the corpus average, in tokens

In wordsSum over query terms: how rare the term is, times how often it appears here, with repeats saturating and long documents discounted. With \(k_1=1.2\) and an average-length document, 1 occurrence scores 1.0, 2 score 1.375, 10 score 1.96, and no count can reach 2.2.

Why it mattersSaturation stops keyword-stuffed or repetitive chunks from dominating. Length normalization stops a 3,000-word page from beating a focused paragraph just because it mentions everything.

$$\text{IDF}(t) = \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\htmlClass{t1}{N} - \htmlClass{t2}{n_t} + 0.5}{\htmlClass{t2}{n_t} + 0.5}\right)$$
\(N\)
number of documents in the corpus
\(n_t\)
document frequency: how many documents contain \(t\) at least once

In wordsRare terms carry information; common ones don't. In a 1,000-document corpus, a term in 1 document gets \(\ln(1 + 999.5/1.5) \approx 6.5\), while a term in 500 gets \(\ln 2 \approx 0.69\). The rare one is worth nearly 10× as much per match. The "+1" inside the log (Lucene's variant) keeps IDF positive even for terms in more than half the documents.

When lexical search wins, and when it loses

BM25 wins

Exact strings that carry the meaning

Error codes (ERR-4012), SKUs, ticket IDs, function names, people's names, rare acronyms, version numbers, quoted phrases. An embedding model sees ERR-4012 as a few subword tokens and places it near every other error code; BM25 sees a rare token with high IDF. BM25 also needs no training, so it doesn't go stale on new jargon. The BEIR benchmark (Thakur et al., 2021) found BM25 a strong zero-shot baseline that many dense retrievers failed to beat outside their training domain.

BM25 loses

Same meaning, different words

"How do I cancel my plan?" against a document that says "terminate your subscription". No shared terms, score zero. Paraphrase, synonyms, cross-lingual queries, and conceptual questions ("why would a refund be blocked?") are where dense retrieval pulls ahead.

Tokenization (the "analyzer") matters as much as the formula: lowercasing, stemming ("refunds" → "refund"), stopword removal, and how you handle punctuation inside codes. If your tokenizer splits ERR-4012 into err and 4012, you lose part of the exact-match advantage. Learned sparse models such as SPLADE (Formal et al., 2021) sit in between: a transformer outputs weights over the vocabulary, including expansion terms that weren't in the text, and the result is served from an ordinary inverted index.

Hybrid search and Reciprocal Rank Fusion

Dense and lexical retrieval fail in different places, so running both and merging the results usually beats either alone. The question is how to merge. Adding raw scores doesn't work: cosine similarities sit in a narrow band like 0.3–0.9, BM25 scores are unbounded and depend on query length and corpus statistics, so one system dominates arbitrarily. You could normalize each list (min-max or z-score) and take a weighted sum, but the normalization is itself fragile from query to query.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, 2009) sidesteps scores entirely and uses only ranks:

$$\text{RRF}(\htmlClass{t1}{d}) = \sum_{\htmlClass{t2}{i} \in \htmlClass{t5}{\mathcal{R}}} \frac{1}{\htmlClass{t3}{k} + \htmlClass{t4}{\text{rank}_i(d)}}$$
\(d\)
a candidate document (chunk)
\(i\)
one retriever's ranked list (e.g. dense, BM25, a second query rewrite)
\(\mathcal{R}\)
the set of ranked lists being fused. A document missing from a list gets no term from it
\(k\)
smoothing constant, 60 in the original paper and in most implementations
\(\text{rank}_i(d)\)
1-based position of \(d\) in list \(i\)

In wordsEach list awards a vote that shrinks slowly with rank. A chunk ranked 1st by dense and 5th by BM25 scores \(1/61 + 1/65 = 0.0318\). A chunk ranked 1st by dense only scores \(1/61 = 0.0164\). Agreement between retrievers beats a single strong opinion.

Why it mattersRRF has no scores to calibrate and one parameter that rarely needs tuning. Larger \(k\) flattens the differences between ranks and rewards consensus; small \(k\) lets a single list's top result dominate. It's what Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, and many vector databases implement for hybrid search.

Pitfall · fusing too shallow

Fuse deep lists (top 50–100 from each retriever), not the top 5. A chunk that's 12th in dense and 3rd in BM25 is exactly the kind of consensus candidate RRF is good at promoting, but only if both lists are long enough to contain it. Then let the reranker pick the final few.

Reranking with cross-encoders

The bi-encoder's strength, encoding query and passage separately, is also its ceiling. The query vector is computed without ever seeing the passage, so subtle interactions ("refund blocked by a dispute" vs "refund for a dispute") are compressed away. A cross-encoder concatenates query and passage into one input and runs a full transformer over both, so every query token attends to every passage token (Nogueira & Cho, 2019, did this with BERT for MS MARCO). It outputs a single relevance score. It's much more accurate, and it can't be precomputed.

$$\htmlClass{t1}{T_{\text{retrieval}}} \approx \htmlClass{t2}{T_{\text{embed}}} + \htmlClass{t3}{T_{\text{search}}} + \frac{\htmlClass{t4}{K}}{\htmlClass{t5}{\text{throughput}_{\text{CE}}}}$$
\(T_{\text{retrieval}}\)
time from query to final ranked context
\(T_{\text{embed}}\)
encode the query once with the bi-encoder (milliseconds)
\(T_{\text{search}}\)
ANN + BM25 lookups, run in parallel; roughly independent of how many candidates you keep
\(K\)
number of candidates sent to the reranker
\(\text{throughput}_{\text{CE}}\)
(query, passage) pairs the cross-encoder scores per second on your hardware, batched

In wordsThe reranker is the only stage whose cost grows with how many candidates you consider. At an illustrative 3,000 pairs/s for a small cross-encoder on a GPU, \(K=100\) costs about 33 ms. Scoring a 10M-chunk corpus that way would take about 56 minutes per query.

Why it mattersThis is why the rule of thumb is "retrieve 50–100, rerank to 5–10". Past about 100 candidates, extra recall is usually small and the latency is real.

Options, from cheapest to most expensive: small open cross-encoders (cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2, about 22M parameters), larger multilingual ones (the BAAI/bge-reranker family), hosted rerank APIs, and LLMs used as rerankers, either scoring passages one at a time or ordering a whole list in one prompt (RankGPT, Sun et al., 2023). ColBERT (Khattab & Zaharia, 2020) sits between bi- and cross-encoders: it stores one vector per token and scores with a cheap "late interaction" (each query token's max similarity over passage tokens), getting much of the cross-encoder's precision with precomputable documents, at the cost of a much larger index.

MLE angle · reranker scores as an abstain signal

Cross-encoder scores are much better calibrated across queries than cosine similarities. Plot the top reranker score for answerable vs unanswerable questions in your golden set; you can often pick a threshold below which the system says "I couldn't find this" instead of letting the LLM improvise from weak context.

Better queries, better chunks

Users write short, ambiguous, conversational queries. Documents are long, formal, and written in a different vocabulary. Two families of techniques close that gap: transform the query at search time, or enrich the chunks at index time.

Query transformations

  • Rewriting (condensing). In a chat, "what about bank transfers?" means nothing on its own. An LLM call rewrites it into a standalone query using the history: "dispute window for bank transfer refunds, ERR-4012". This one is close to mandatory for conversational RAG.
  • Multi-query. Generate 3–5 paraphrases, retrieve for each, and fuse the lists with RRF. This raises recall on ambiguous queries at the cost of N× retrieval work (cheap) plus one LLM call.
  • HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings, Gao et al., 2022). Ask the LLM to write a plausible answer, then embed that instead of the question. Answers look more like documents than questions do, so they land closer in embedding space. The risk: if the hypothetical answer contains invented specifics, it can steer retrieval toward the wrong documents. It helps most with weak or untuned embedders.
  • Decomposition. "Did the refund policy change before or after the dispute-window change?" needs two retrievals. Break it into sub-questions, retrieve for each, then answer over the union. Step-back prompting (Zheng et al., 2023) goes the other way: retrieve for a more general version of the question as well.
  • Routing and self-query. Classify the query to pick an index (docs, tickets, code) or a different tool entirely (SQL for "how many refunds last month"), and extract structured filters: "refund policy changes in 2024" → year = 2024.

Each transformation adds an LLM call, typically 100–500 ms with a small model. Add them one at a time, and keep one only if it moves recall on your golden set.

Contextual retrieval

A chunk that reads "The window is 7 days for card payments and 3 days for bank transfers" is ambiguous on its own: which window, which product? Anthropic's contextual retrieval (2024) prepends a short, chunk-specific explanation generated by an LLM that sees the whole document, and indexes the combined text in both the embedding index and BM25:


CONTEXT_PROMPT = """<document>

</document>

<chunk>

</chunk>

def contextualize(client, model, document, chunk):
    resp = client.chat.completions.create(
        model=model, temperature=0, max_tokens=120,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content": CONTEXT_PROMPT.format(document=document, chunk=chunk)}],

Anthropic reported that, on their evaluation sets, contextual embeddings reduced the top-20 retrieval failure rate by 35%, adding contextual BM25 brought that to 49%, and adding a reranker to 67%. The cost is one LLM call per chunk at ingest. Because the full document is the same prefix for all of its chunks, prompt caching makes this much cheaper than it sounds. Treat their numbers as evidence that the idea is worth testing, not as what you'll get.

Parent-document (small-to-big) retrieval

Resolve the chunk-size tension by using two sizes. Index small units (a sentence or ~100–200 tokens) so that matching is precise, but store a pointer from each to its parent section (~1,000–2,000 tokens). At query time, search the small units and put the parents in the context, deduplicating when several hits share a parent. A variant, sentence-window retrieval, returns the hit plus N sentences on either side.

Metadata filtering

Filters on metadata (product, region, language, date, document type, and above all access groups) shrink the search space to what's allowed and relevant. There are two places to apply them:

  • Post-filter: retrieve top-k, then drop disallowed results. Simple, but if the filter is selective (1% of the corpus), your top-100 may contain one usable result.
  • Pre-filter / filtered search: apply the filter during the index traversal. This is correct, but graph indexes can lose connectivity under very selective filters, so vector databases use special strategies or fall back to brute force on small filtered sets. Find out what yours does, and measure recall with filters on.

Assembling the context window

You have 5–10 good chunks. How you put them in the prompt still matters.

Deduplicate and merge

Overlapping chunks, near-duplicate documents, and multi-query retrieval all produce repeats. Drop chunks whose text is contained in another selected chunk, collapse near-duplicates (high embedding similarity), and merge adjacent chunks from the same document into one passage so the model reads continuous text with one citation.

Order, and the lost-in-the-middle effect

Practical consequences: put the strongest chunk first, consider putting the second strongest last, and send fewer, better chunks rather than more. If chunks come from the same document, presenting them in document order can help the model follow the argument. Test both orderings.

Budget the tokens

Retrieval metrics

These need a labeled set: for each query, which chunks (or documents) are relevant, optionally with graded relevance (0 = irrelevant, 1 = related, 2 = useful, 3 = answers it).

$$\text{P@}\htmlClass{t1}{k} = \frac{\lvert \htmlClass{t2}{\text{Rel}} \cap \htmlClass{t3}{\text{Ret}_k} \rvert}{\htmlClass{t1}{k}}, \qquad \text{R@}\htmlClass{t1}{k} = \frac{\lvert \htmlClass{t2}{\text{Rel}} \cap \htmlClass{t3}{\text{Ret}_k} \rvert}{\lvert \htmlClass{t2}{\text{Rel}} \rvert}$$
\(k\)
cutoff: how many top results you look at
\(\text{Rel}\)
set of relevant items for this query (from your labels)
\(\text{Ret}_k\)
the top \(k\) items the system returned

In wordsPrecision: what fraction of what you showed was relevant. Recall: what fraction of what's relevant you showed. Average both over all queries.

Why it mattersFor RAG, recall@k at the k you actually send to the LLM is the headline number: if the answer isn't in the top 5, the model never sees it. Precision matters second, because irrelevant chunks cost tokens and distract.

$$\text{MRR} = \frac{1}{\htmlClass{t1}{\lvert \mathcal{Q} \rvert}} \sum_{\htmlClass{t2}{q} \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{1}{\htmlClass{t3}{\text{rank}_q}}$$
\(\lvert\mathcal{Q}\rvert\)
number of queries in the evaluation set
\(q\)
one query
\(\text{rank}_q\)
position of the first relevant result (the term is 0 if none appears in the top \(k\))

In wordsHow far down does the user have to read before hitting something useful? First place scores 1, second 0.5, third 0.33.

Why it mattersGood for single-answer lookups. It ignores everything after the first hit, so it can't tell "one good chunk" from "five good chunks".

$$\text{DCG@}\htmlClass{t4}{k} = \sum_{i=1}^{\htmlClass{t4}{k}} \frac{2^{\htmlClass{t1}{\text{rel}_i}} - 1}{\htmlClass{t2}{\log_2(i+1)}}, \qquad \text{nDCG@}\htmlClass{t4}{k} = \frac{\text{DCG@}\htmlClass{t4}{k}}{\htmlClass{t3}{\text{IDCG@}k}}$$
\(\text{rel}_i\)
graded relevance of the result at position \(i\) (e.g. 0–3). The gain \(2^{\text{rel}}-1\) makes a "3" worth 7 and a "1" worth 1; a linear gain of just \(\text{rel}\) is also common, so say which one you report
\(\log_2(i+1)\)
position discount: 1 at the top, 1.58 at rank 2, 2 at rank 3, 3.32 at rank 10
\(\text{IDCG@}k\)
DCG of the ideal ordering: all judged items sorted by relevance, including relevant ones the system never returned
\(k\)
cutoff

In wordsAdd up the value of each result, discounted by how far down it is, then divide by the best score possible, which puts it on a 0–1 scale.

Why it mattersThe only metric here that uses graded relevance and full ordering, which is why it's the metric BEIR and MTEB retrieval report (Järvelin & Kekäläinen, 2002). Use it to compare rerankers, where order within the top 10 is the whole point.

Generation metrics

Given the retrieved context and the answer, four questions cover most of it. The RAGAS framework (Es et al., 2023) popularized names and LLM-judge recipes for them:

  • Faithfulness (groundedness): is every claim in the answer supported by the context?
  • Answer relevance: does the answer address the question that was asked? RAGAS estimates it by generating questions from the answer and measuring their similarity to the original.
  • Context precision: are the relevant chunks ranked near the top of what was retrieved?
  • Context recall: does the retrieved context contain everything needed for the reference answer? Compute it by checking each claim of a reference answer against the context.
$$\text{Faithfulness}(\htmlClass{t4}{a}) = \frac{\big\lvert \{\, \htmlClass{t1}{c} \in \htmlClass{t2}{\mathcal{C}(a)} : \htmlClass{t3}{\text{ctx}} \models \htmlClass{t1}{c} \,\} \big\rvert}{\lvert \htmlClass{t2}{\mathcal{C}(a)} \rvert}$$
\(a\)
the generated answer
\(\mathcal{C}(a)\)
the atomic claims extracted from the answer (an LLM splits "the window is 7 days for cards and 3 for transfers" into two claims)
\(c\)
one claim
\(\text{ctx} \models c\)
the retrieved context entails (supports) the claim, judged by an LLM or an NLI model

In wordsThe fraction of the answer's factual claims you could verify by reading the sources. 1.0 means nothing was made up, though not necessarily that the answer is complete or correct.

Why it mattersFaithfulness and correctness are different. If the context is wrong (a stale doc), a perfectly faithful answer is wrong too. Track both: faithfulness against the context, correctness against a reference answer.

Caveat · LLM judges are instruments, so calibrate them

Every metric above except the retrieval ones usually runs on an LLM judge, and judges have known biases: they prefer longer answers, answers in the first position, and answers written by their own model family, and claim extraction is itself noisy. Before trusting a judge, have humans label 50–100 examples, measure agreement with the judge (Module 13), and fix the judge prompt until agreement is acceptable. Re-check whenever you change the judge model.

Building a golden evaluation set

You need a few hundred (question, relevant source, reference answer) triples eventually, and 50 to start. Sources, in order of value:

  1. Real user questions from logs, search bars, support tickets, or Slack. They have the right vocabulary, length, and ambiguity. Label which documents answer them.
  2. Synthetic questions: sample chunks, ask an LLM to write a question that the chunk answers, keep the chunk as the label. Fast and cheap, but biased. The questions tend to reuse the chunk's exact wording, which flatters BM25 and any retriever, so instruct the model to paraphrase, and discard questions with high word overlap with their source.
  3. Adversarial and edge cases: unanswerable questions (the system should abstain), questions whose answer changed between document versions, multi-hop questions, questions using IDs and codes.

MLE angle · label documents or spans, not chunk IDs

If your labels say "chunk 4417 is relevant", they break the moment you change chunk size, which is the first thing you'll want to tune. Label the source document (or a character span within it), and score a retrieved chunk as relevant if it comes from, or overlaps, a labeled span. Your eval set then survives re-chunking, re-embedding, and index changes.

Pitfall · tiny eval sets, confident conclusions

With 50 questions, a recall@5 of 0.80 has a 95% confidence interval of roughly ±0.11. A 3-point gap between two configurations is noise. Compare systems on the same questions (paired), bootstrap the difference, and grow the set before betting on small wins.

Beyond the basic pipeline: advanced patterns and production

Agentic and iterative RAG

The pipeline above retrieves once, then answers. For multi-hop questions, a better shape is a loop: the model decides whether to search, what to search for, reads the results, and searches again until it has enough (the ReAct pattern, covered in Module 12). Variants train or prompt the model to judge its own retrieval: Self-RAG (Asai et al., 2023) generates reflection tokens deciding when to retrieve and whether passages are relevant; FLARE (Jiang et al., 2023) retrieves again when the model's next sentence has low-confidence tokens. The cost is more LLM calls and less predictable latency. The benefit is that retrieval can follow the reasoning instead of guessing everything from the first query.

GraphRAG

Some questions aren't about any one passage: "what are the main themes across all 2,000 incident reports?" Microsoft's GraphRAG (Edge et al., 2024) uses an LLM at ingest to extract entities and relationships into a knowledge graph, clusters it into communities, and pre-writes a summary of each community. "Global" questions are answered from those summaries. It helps with sensemaking over a whole corpus; it's much more expensive to index (LLM calls over all the text) and isn't a replacement for chunk retrieval on specific lookups.

Long context vs RAG, and caching

As context windows grew, "just paste everything" became viable for more corpora. The trade-off is mostly economic and operational:

  • A small, static, non-permissioned corpus (under roughly 100K tokens) can live in the prompt. Prompt caching, where the provider or engine reuses the KV cache of an identical prefix (Module 08), cuts the repeated cost and time to first token.
  • A big, changing, or permissioned corpus needs retrieval. Even with a 1M-token window, 10,000 queries a day × 1M tokens is 10 billion input tokens a day.
  • The combination often works best: retrieve generously (whole sections or documents instead of tiny chunks) into a long-context model, so retrieval only has to be roughly right.

Semantic caches, which reuse an earlier answer when a new query embeds close to an old one, can cut cost for repetitive traffic. They're also an easy way to serve a stale or wrong answer to a subtly different question, so key them on normalized query plus the relevant filters (user permissions included) and keep a tight similarity threshold.

Production concerns

Index refresh

Ingest is a data pipeline, not a script

Re-ingest on change events or on a schedule, skipping unchanged documents by content hash. Handle deletes explicitly with tombstones, or deleted docs keep answering. Changing the embedding model or chunker means rebuilding the whole index: build the new one alongside, evaluate, then switch traffic (blue/green).

Permissions

ACLs are enforced in retrieval

Copy access lists from the source system into chunk metadata and filter on the requesting user's groups in every search. Sync permission changes as fast as content changes. A system prompt saying "don't reveal confidential docs" is not access control.

Latency budget

Know where the time goes

Illustrative per-stage costs: query rewrite with a small LLM 100–400 ms; query embedding 5–30 ms; ANN and BM25 5–50 ms in parallel; reranking 100 candidates 30–200 ms on a GPU; LLM time to first token 200–1,000 ms; then generation. Stream the answer, and run retrieval while you're still rewriting if you can.

Cost

Generation tokens dominate

Embedding a 10M-token corpus is a one-off 10M tokens. Sending 5,000 context tokens on each of a million monthly queries is 5 billion tokens every month. Fewer, better chunks (via reranking) are usually the biggest cost lever, followed by caching.

MLE angle · log the whole trace

For every request, log the original and rewritten query, filters, retrieved chunk IDs with scores from each retriever, the reranker's scores, the exact prompt, the answer, citations, latency per stage, and any user feedback. That trace is your debugger, your source of new golden questions, and your regression suite when you change a model. Respect the same ACL and retention rules as the source documents; the logs contain their text.

            buf.append(line)
    if "".join(buf).strip():
        out.append((" > ".join(path), "\n".join(buf)))
    return out

def split_recursive(text, size, seps=SEPARATORS):
    """Pieces no longer than `size` chars, splitting on the largest separator that works."""
    if len(text) <= size:
        return [text]
    for i, sep in enumerate(seps):
        if sep in text:
            parts = text.split(sep)
            parts = [p + sep for p in parts[:-1]] + [parts[-1]]        # keep the separator
            out = []
            for p in parts:
                out.extend([p] if len(p) <= size else split_recursive(p, size, seps[i + 1:]))
            return out
    return [text[j:j + size] for j in range(0, len(text), size)]       # no separator left: hard cut

def pack(pieces, size=1200, overlap=150):
    """Greedily pack pieces into chunks of at most `size` chars, carrying up to `overlap` chars forward."""
    chunks, cur = [], []
    for p in pieces:
        if cur and sum(map(len, cur)) + len(p) > size:
            chunks.append("".join(cur))
            tail = []
            while cur and sum(map(len, tail)) + len(cur[-1]) <= overlap:
                tail.insert(0, cur.pop())
            cur = tail
            while cur and sum(map(len, cur)) + len(p) > size:
                cur.pop(0)
        cur.append(p)
    if cur:
        chunks.append("".join(cur))
    return [c.strip() for c in chunks if c.strip()]

def load_chunks(folder, size=1200, overlap=150):
    chunks = []
    for f in sorted(pathlib.Path(folder).rglob("*.md")):
        for heading, body in sections(f.read_text(encoding="utf-8")):
            for text in pack(split_recursive(body, size), size, overlap):
                chunks.append({"id": len(chunks), "source": str(f), "heading": heading, "text": text})
    return chunks

def dense_search(query, k=50):
    q = embedder.encode([QUERY_PREFIX + query], normalize_embeddings=True).astype("float32")
    _, ids = index.search(q, k)
    return [int(i) for i in ids[0] if i != -1]

def bm25_search(query, k=50):
    scores = bm25.get_scores(tokenize(query))
    top = np.argsort(-scores)[:k]
    return [int(i) for i in top if scores[i] > 0]

def rrf(rankings, k=60):
    fused = {}
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, doc_id in enumerate(ranking, start=1):
            fused[doc_id] = fused.get(doc_id, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank)
    return sorted(fused, key=fused.get, reverse=True)

reranker = CrossEncoder(RERANK_MODEL)

def rerank(query, ids, top_n=6):
    if not ids:
        return []
    scores = reranker.predict([(query, passages[i]) for i in ids], batch_size=32)
    order = np.argsort(-scores)[:top_n]
    return [ids[j] for j in order]

def retrieve(query, mode="hybrid+rerank", k=50, top_n=6):
    if mode == "dense":
        return dense_search(query, k)[:top_n]
    if mode == "bm25":
        return bm25_search(query, k)[:top_n]
    fused = rrf([dense_search(query, k), bm25_search(query, k)])
    if mode == "hybrid":
        return fused[:top_n]
    return rerank(query, fused[:k], top_n)       # "hybrid+rerank"

With RRF and \(k = 60\): chunk X is rank 2 in the dense list and rank 4 in the BM25 list. Chunk Y is rank 1 in the dense list and absent from the BM25 list. Which ranks higher after fusion?

  1. Y, because first place in any list always wins
  2. X, with about 0.0318 (1/62 + 1/64) vs Y's 0.0164 (1/61)
  3. X, with 1/2 + 1/4 = 0.75
  4. They tie, because RRF only uses the best rank
X gets 1/62 + 1/64 = 0.01613 + 0.01563 = 0.0318. Y gets only 1/61 = 0.0164 because it earns nothing from BM25. With \(k=60\), rank differences near the top are small, so appearing in both lists roughly doubles the score. Option C forgets the constant \(k\).

Binary relevance. Your top 3 results are [relevant, not relevant, relevant], and those are the only 2 relevant documents. What is nDCG@3?

  1. 0.67, because 2 of 3 are relevant
  2. 1.00, because both relevant documents were found
  3. 0.75
  4. About 0.92
DCG = 1/log₂2 + 0 + 1/log₂4 = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5. The ideal order [rel, rel, not] gives IDCG = 1 + 1/log₂3 = 1 + 0.631 = 1.631. nDCG = 1.5 / 1.631 ≈ 0.92. Recall@3 is 1.0 and P@3 is 0.67, but nDCG also penalizes the second relevant document for sitting at rank 3 instead of rank 2.

Users search for ERR-4012. Dense retrieval returns pages about ERR-4021 and ERR-4102; BM25 returns the right page first. Why?

  1. The embedding places near-identical codes close together in semantic space, while BM25 requires the exact token, and a rare token has a high IDF
  2. BM25 understands the meaning of error codes better than embeddings
  3. The embedding model's context window is too short for the query
  4. Cosine similarity cannot represent numbers
An embedding model sees "ERR-4012" as a few subword pieces and maps it near every other error-code-shaped string. That's good for meaning and bad for identity. BM25 matches the exact token, and because it appears in very few documents, its IDF is high. That's the classic case for hybrid search.

An IVF index has \(n_{\text{list}} = 4096\). You raise \(n_{\text{probe}}\) from 8 to 64. What happens?

  1. Recall drops and latency drops
  2. Recall rises, since fewer true neighbours sit in unscanned cells, and the list-scanning work grows about 8×
  3. Index memory grows about 8×
  4. Nothing, unless you retrain the k-means centroids
\(n_{\text{probe}}\) is a query-time setting: the index and its memory are unchanged. Scanning 8× more lists does about 8× the distance computations in the second term of the cost formula. Recall rises because neighbours just across cell boundaries are now inside a scanned cell, with diminishing returns as \(n_{\text{probe}}\) grows.

What does chunk overlap actually buy you?

  1. Fewer chunks to embed
  2. A smaller index, through compression
  3. A fact that straddles a chunk boundary still appears whole in at least one chunk, at the cost of about \(s/(s-o)\) times more storage and some duplicate hits
  4. It lets the embedding model attend across chunks
Without overlap, a sentence cut at a boundary is split across two chunks and neither embeds its full meaning. Overlap re-includes the tail of the previous chunk. It increases the number of chunks (by \(s/(s-o)\)) and can return near-duplicate results, so 10–20% is the usual range. Embeddings never attend across chunks; that's what late chunking changes.

Why not use the cross-encoder for all of retrieval over 5 million chunks, since it's more accurate?

  1. Cross-encoders are less accurate than bi-encoders on long passages
  2. Cross-encoders can't process documents over 100 tokens
  3. Cross-encoder outputs don't fit in FAISS
  4. It needs a full forward pass per (query, chunk) pair at query time, and nothing can be precomputed. 5 million passes per query takes many minutes; a bi-encoder encodes the query once and uses precomputed vectors
The cross-encoder's accuracy comes from reading query and passage together, which is exactly what prevents precomputation. At an illustrative 3,000 pairs/s, 5M pairs is about 28 minutes per query. So you retrieve about 100 candidates cheaply and rerank only those, which takes tens of milliseconds.
